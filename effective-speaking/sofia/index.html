How to Speak Effectively: end project

Be the Screen

A 12-minute talk on how a picture becomes numbers. I gave it to 16 holiday campers, aged 8 to 12. They sat in a circle on the floor. No slides. The campers were the screen.

I'm Sofia. I run holiday camps for mixed ages. My hardest speaking is 12 minutes to an 8-year-old and a 12-year-old at once. The 12-year-old is bored by minute two. The 8-year-old is lost by minute three. So my rule is simple. Nobody sits still for more than two minutes without saying or doing something. My talk is a game with words in between.

The camp, the campers and Jun, my co-trainer, are made up, like me. The facts about screens and pixels are real, and the sources are at the end.

The live talk

Who16 campers, aged 8 to 12. Eight older and younger buddy pairs.
WhereA community centre activity room. No projector, no chairs.
WhenDay 2 of a 5-day coding camp in the June school holidays, 10.15 a.m.
Length11 minutes 51 seconds, then snack
My spotThe middle of the circle, mostly. The edge for games.
HelperJun, my co-trainer, with the cards and a tally sheet

The room

The mat is two sheets of mahjong paper taped together. It has an 8 by 8 grid in marker. The tape grid is masking tape on the floor. Cards are A4 card, white on one side and black on the other.

The talk, in chunks

Every chunk of me talking is short. Each one ends with the campers doing something. The times are from the recording.

  1. 0:00TalkThe freeze signal and the mystery card full of numbers. My promise: "Before snack time, every one of you will read this picture."
  2. 0:51TalkWho has zoomed right into a photo? What did you see? Squares, called pixels: picture elements.
  3. 2:18DoBe the screen. Nine campers stand on the tape grid with cards. The rest are the computer and call out the rows. We make a T. Mia, 8, calls out an X.
  4. 4:18TalkThe counting rule: count the squares in a row. The first number is always white. A stand-up, sit-down check.
  5. 5:06DoDecode the mystery card on the floor mat together. Nur reads row two. Aryan cracks the zero rows.
  6. 6:56DoBuddy time, 2 minutes. Big buddies read, small buddies colour. Early finishers make their own.
  7. 9:08TalkFreeze. Phones send pictures as numbers. Fax machines used this counting trick. Colour screens mix red, green and blue light.
  8. 10:33DoThe reveal. The whole circle reads the mystery card. It's a ghost.
  9. 11:22TalkThree things you can do now. A word for three campers by name. Freeze, and snack.

The speaking techniques

  • A call and answer for attention. I say "Pixels", they say "Freeze". It saves my voice, and it makes quiet a game, not a telling-off.
  • A promise in camper words. "Before snack time" means more to an 8-year-old than "in 12 minutes".
  • A mystery at the start that only gets solved at the end. The ghost card goes up twice.
  • The key rule: "the first number is always white". I say it twice, then check it with bodies.
  • Standing in the middle of a circle. Everyone is close, but someone always sees my back. I turn every two or three sentences.
  • Getting down to their height. I crouch next to a camper who answers. The answer is theirs, not mine.
  • Voice: loud and bright for "Pixels!", then nearly a whisper for "Don't say it yet." The change gets more attention than volume does.
  • Waiting. After the zero-row question, I waited five seconds. More hands went up at five than at two.

Mixed ages: who does what

  • Big buddies read the numbers. Small buddies colour. Reading "zero, two, one, two" is harder than colouring. The colouring is where the picture appears.
  • The quiet camper. Nur hadn't spoken all day. I asked her to say her answer again, louder. Then I gave her the marker. She stood in the middle, and I stepped back.
  • The one who calls out. Aryan answers everything first. I gave him the hardest question, the zero rows. Then a job: reading for Mia.
  • The early finisher. Ethan finished buddy time in under a minute. He got a blank card and a new job, making a picture for Nur's pair. Later, a challenge: add a colour number to the code.

Play the decoder

This is buddy time, on screen. Pick a card. Each row's numbers count the squares: white, then black, then white, and so on. The first number is always white. Tap squares to colour them, or drag across a few. A tick appears when a row is right.

Try "Draw your own" and see your numbers change as you draw. Then read them to someone with paper and a pencil. Can they draw your picture without seeing it?

My pre-talk routine

I don't get nervous with children. I get nervous that the room will go flat. So my routine is about energy and setup.

  1. 30 minutes before: tape the grid, lay out the mat, count the cards. After this talk, the cards also go on the tape squares before anyone arrives.
  2. 20 minutes: practise the "Pixels, Freeze" signal with Jun. Then two of us can show it.
  3. 15 minutes: a voice warm-up with whoever is early. Lip trills become a motorbike race round the circle. Then gentle humming. Nobody finds it odd when everyone does it.
  4. 5 minutes: water. Say my promise out loud once, to the empty mat.
  5. Just before: three slow breaths in the corridor, and a big stretch. Then I walk in clapping.

Feedback and reflection

Sticker votes from the campers

After snack, each camper put a sticker on a poster by the door. 16 campers, 16 stickers per question. I kept the questions to faces and words an 8-year-old can read.

Could you read a secret picture?
Was being the screen fun?
Was the talking part too long?

Teal is the happy answer: yes, or not too long. Yellow is in between. Coral is not yet, or too long. The counts are made up, like the camp.

Seven campers said the talking was at least a bit long. The two "yes" stickers were both 12-year-olds. That matches the replay. I talked for over half the time.

My reflection, rubric line by rubric line

Clear, audible, well-paced voice

Mostly. Nobody asked me to repeat anything, and I never shouted, thanks to the freeze signal. But I rush when I'm excited. My TV line raced out at about 165 words a minute. It had two big numbers in one breath. For 8-year-olds, one number is plenty. The quiet voice for the secret was my best voice moment.

Confident posture and purposeful gestures

The crouches worked. Every time a camper answered, I got down to their height and then stepped back. But twice, in the middle, I stood still with my back to one side. A circle needs a speaker who keeps turning.

Logical flow and clear key messages

This is the strongest part. One rule: "the first number is always white". I said it twice, checked it with bodies, and used it three times. 13 of 16 decoded their card. The mystery card frames the whole talk: shown at the start, solved at the end.

Holds attention and responds to the audience

Mixed. I used names, gave roles, and changed plan when two campers drifted. But the replay shows I talked for over half the talk. Campers spoke aloud for under a minute. Most of that was chorus. The game parts held them. My talking parts held most of them.

What I would change

Less of me. I would cut the TV numbers and the fax machine. Buddy time would get another minute. The fax line got the biggest laugh. That was only because nobody knew what a fax was. A camper could explain the phone part instead of me.

I'd also fix the dead time. The cards go on the tape squares before the talk. Jun colours rows five to eight of the ghost while I talk. Then the reveal is quick.

And one gap in the content: our code only does black and white. Ethan's colour challenge was the best question of the day. I left it for the afternoon. For a group of only 10 to 12-year-olds, I would build it into the talk.
