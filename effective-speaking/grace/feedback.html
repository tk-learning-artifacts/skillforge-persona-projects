  • "Which password manager? Is there a free one?" (4 cards say something like this.)
  • "Where is the word list?" (2 cards. It was on the handout, but nobody turned it over until I said so.)

  • "Can my son use the dice method for his school account?" (1 card)

Four parents asking about password managers is a gap in my talk. I named them in one line and moved on. I didn't want to recommend a product from the front of a school room. So the week after, I sent a short note through the class teacher. It explains what a password manager does, and says CSA suggests a reputable one. It suggests they ask their phone or browser maker what is built in. For the school account question, I said to check the school's own password rules first.

My reflection, rubric line by rubric line

The times link to my notes on the recording page.

Clear, audible, well-paced voice

Audible, yes: nobody asked me to repeat anything, and Ben heard every word from the back row. Pace, mostly. Most of my lines sit in my 120 to 150 words a minute band. The three ideas are slower, on purpose. But my first four lines and my answer to the bank question are the fastest in the talk. Both times I was anxious. I counted 5 fillers in 12 minutes, which is fewer than I feared. Two of them came in those same fast stretches. So the fix is not "stop saying um". The fix is to slow down when I feel caught out. I will practise answering a hard question with a breath first.

Confident posture and purposeful gestures

Standing without a podium helped. I could step towards the father who asked about his bank, and turn to the side that looked lost. Most of my gestures did a job: counting doors, a thousand and a million, counting the three things they can now do. Two did not. I turned my back for 4 seconds to open the calculator. And Farah counted me touching my glasses six times in the first two minutes. That is nerves, and it settled once the parents started voting.

Logical flow and clear key messages

This is the strongest part. The talk has one promise, three ideas, and each comes round three times. The vote at the start sets up a puzzle, and the "clever tricks" slide answers it. Both observers could say the three ideas without notes. The exit cards show every parent picked one of the three. Nobody wrote anything else.

Holds attention and responds to the audience

The parents spoke 7 times in 12 minutes. That's a lot for a talk. The pair talk at 8:11 changed the room, and the questions got braver after it. I stopped for the frowns at 4:25 and explained the sums another way. Where it dipped was the sums themselves, which Ben noticed too. Three big numbers in a row is a lot for a Saturday. Next time I will show one number and let the calculator do the rest.

What I will do differently

  1. Put "add a second lock" first. It's the quickest win, and the parents chose it.
  2. Answer hard questions with a breath first, then the answer at my normal pace.
  3. Open the calculator before I start, and stand facing the room while it runs.
  4. One big number on screen, not three.
  5. Plan for languages: a handout in Chinese, Malay and Tamil, or a parent volunteer.
  6. Roll real dice at the front, with two parents, so "random" is something they see.

My speaking notes for next time

This module also asks for a personal style guide. Here is mine, as short as I could make it.

  • With students, I can be quick. With adults, I slow down for the first minute, because they take longer to settle.
  • Ask a question early, and wait five seconds. The answer that comes after the silence is better than the one I would have accepted at two.
  • Turn towards confusion, not away from it.
  • One idea per slide, and say the sentence aloud.
  • When someone challenges me, breathe, thank them, and answer slowly.
  • End on what they can now do.
