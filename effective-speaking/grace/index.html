How to Speak Effectively: end project

Long beats clever

A 12-minute talk on how passwords get guessed. I gave it live to 21 parents of a Sec 1 coding class, on a Saturday morning.

I'm Grace. I've spent twenty years in classrooms, and now I mentor new trainers. I talk to students every day. A room of parents is harder. They are adults who chose to give up a Saturday. Many feel unsure about technology, and nobody likes feeling slow in front of other parents. So I picked a technical topic they could use that night, and I planned the talk around questions and pauses, the same as a good lesson.

The parents, the two trainee observers and the morning itself are made up, like me. The facts about passwords are real, and the sources are at the end.

Why this topic, for this room

At the start of term, three parents asked me the same thing. Their child had to make passwords for the school's accounts. What makes a good one? Most of us were taught "a capital, a number and a symbol". That advice turns out to be weak, and the reason is a nice piece of counting. It's technical, but you can explain it with a school locker. And it ends with something to do at the kitchen table.

The promise and the three ideas

In 12 minutes, you can make a password a computer can't guess in your lifetime.
  1. Long beats clever. Every extra character multiplies the work. Clever swaps are on the guess list.
  2. One password, one account. One leak should open one door, not all of them.
  3. Add a second lock. Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA), starting with your email.

Each idea comes back three times: when I first explain it, on its own slide, and in the closing summary. Patrick Winston calls this cycling. He says that at any moment, some of your audience has drifted off. So you go round again.

The techniques, and where to see them

  • An opening promise, not a joke. Winston calls it an empowerment promise. Mine is on slide 2.
  • Wait time. After the vote, I counted five seconds in my head. Mary Budd Rowe found that teachers who wait 3 seconds or more get longer, better answers. Most of us wait far less.
  • Verbal punctuation. "That's the first half done." "Idea two." People who drifted off know where to rejoin.
  • A fence: what the idea is not. "Long" means long and random. A sentence you would say is not random.
  • One idea per slide, with very few words. I say the sentences. The slide shows the picture or the number.
  • A story. My own email sending adverts for sunglasses. Every story in the talk serves one idea.
  • Voice: I slowed right down for the three ideas. On the recording, they are my slowest lines.
  • Body: one finger, two fingers for the two doors. Hands close, then wide, for a thousand and a million. I turned my whole body towards the side that looked lost.
  • An ending on what they can now do, then a word about the room, in place of "thank you".

The handout

The front has the three ideas, in big type. Long beats clever. One password, one account. Add a second lock.

The back has the dice method. Roll five dice. Read them as a 5-digit number. Find it on the EFF long word list and write down the word. Do it six times. Use the passphrase for one account only, and never write it on anything you post or hand in. At the bottom is the web address of the EFF word list.

Try the calculator from the talk

I used this live at 4:45. Parents shouted out a number of words, and the room watched the time change. You never type a password here. You only choose a kind and a length.

6 words

Both speeds are numbers I picked to keep the sums easy. They are not measurements. Real speeds depend on how the website scrambled its passwords. The sums only hold for random passwords. A password you made up yourself is guessed much sooner, because people make them up in similar ways.

My pre-talk routine

I've spoken to rooms for twenty years and I still get nervous with adults. This is what I did that morning. It takes about 30 minutes, most of it quiet.

  1. 30 minutes before: sit in the back row and click through every slide. If I can't read it from there, it changes.
  2. 25 minutes: open the calculator in a second tab. (I forgot this. See 4:45 on the recording.)
  3. 20 minutes: a 5-minute voice warm-up in the empty room. Gentle humming, lip trills, then "Good morning, everyone" to the far wall. Water, not coffee.
  4. 10 minutes: say the opening and the closing aloud, once each. I never learn a talk word for word, but I know my first and last lines.
  5. 5 minutes: stand at the door and greet parents as they come in. A room of people I've said hello to is less scary than a room of strangers.
  6. Just before: three slow breaths. In for 4 counts, out for 6. Then wait for quiet before the first word.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) suggests gentle humming to warm up, drinking water, and not whispering, which strains the voice too.

What I would change

The talk was in English only, with an English handout. At least three parents spoke to each other in Mandarin during the pair talk. A handout in Chinese, Malay and Tamil, or a parent volunteer to translate the three ideas, would have reached more of the room. I would plan that before anything else next time.

I also leaned on "random" without showing it. The dice are on the handout, but nobody rolled a die in the room. A 60-second dice roll with two parents at the front would make "random" real, and it would cost me the story, which is the right trade.

And I rushed the start. My first four lines were the fastest on the recording. The rest is on the feedback page.

  • Composition rules make people respond "in very predictable ways" (Password, Password1, Password1!), and length is "a primary factor": NIST SP 800-63B-4, Appendix A.
  • At least 15 characters when a password is the only factor, no composition rules, no forced periodic changes, change after a compromise: NIST SP 800-63B-4, section 3.1.1.2, published July 2025 (NIST CSRC).
  • At least 12 characters, long and random, and 2FA: CSA, Enable 2FA and Use Strong Passphrases.
  • Don't reuse passwords across accounts, and use a reputable password manager: SPF and CSA advisory, 21 May 2025.
