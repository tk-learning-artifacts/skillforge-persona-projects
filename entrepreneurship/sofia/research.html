Market research

Ten problems, one pick, ten interviews and a pre-order sheet. All in the first four days of camp.

The children, parents and their words are made up, like me. So are the counts.

The problem hunt

Monday, 9.15 am. Each team had 10 minutes and a pile of sticky notes. The challenge: "Name a problem a real person had this week." The wall filled up fast. We kept 10.

  1. Little brothers and sisters get bored when the family has to wait. (Picked.)
  2. Water bottles get mixed up at camp.
  3. The playground slide is too hot at noon.
  4. Shoes go missing at the pool.
  5. Nobody wants to play board games after dinner.
  6. Pencils are always blunt.
  7. Grown-ups look at phones during dinner.
  8. The lift takes ages in the morning.
  9. Forgetting what homework is due.
  10. No shade at the bus stop.

Each camper got three dot stickers to vote. Number 1 won with 19 dots. Arjun, 12, said half the room had a little cousin or sibling like that. They melt down in queues. Our How Might We question:

How might we help little kids have fun while their family waits, without a screen?

The quiet ones voted too. That is why I use dots, not hands up.

How we interviewed

  • Monday and Tuesday, at drop-off and pick-up. Ten interviews.
  • Campers worked in pairs. One asked, one wrote. First names only.
  • A camp helper stood with every pair. Campers only spoke to parents at our door.
  • For the 4 children, Ms Farah sat with them. Questions were short and the chats lasted 3 minutes.
  • The rule I gave them: talk about the last time, not about our idea. No pitching until the end.

Our 5 questions for parents

  1. Tell us about the last time you waited somewhere with your child.
  2. How long was it? Where?
  3. What did your child do? What did you do?
  4. How did it end?
  5. What did you last pay for a small thing to keep your child busy?

For the children: "Where do you have to wait? What do you do?"

What we found

  • Waits were 15 to 40 minutes: queues, clinics and the MRT.
  • 3 of 6 parents gave a phone the last time. All 3 didn't like how it ended.
  • Crayons and small bits get lost under tables. Only cards, nothing loose.
  • The waiting children are 4 to 7. Many can't read yet.
  • Grown-ups want to play too. Most games need 2 players.
  • Brothers and sisters of different ages need games where age doesn't decide the winner.
  • Last small buys cost $3 to $8. Three of five were $3 or $4.

What we got wrong

The games are for kids our age, 8 and up.
The waiting kids are 4 to 7. Many can't read.
Parents want quiet games so kids leave them alone.
Parents want something they can play too.
$8 sounds fine. (The Shop team's first guess.)
Most small buys were $3 to $5. $8 was the top.
Every family with little kids will buy one.
Ms Anna wouldn't. Some families don't wait much.

The pre-order sheet

Interviews tell you about a problem. A sheet tells you if people will act. On Tuesday, before anyone saw the sheet, the Shop team set a target: 15 decks.

  • Wednesday pick-up: a table at the door. On it, a version 1 sample deck and the sheet.
  • The sheet said: "Queue Quest, $4 a deck. Pay on Friday at the showcase. No money now."
  • Families wrote a first name and how many decks. Nobody paid anything.
  • The sheet closed at Thursday pick-up.
  • Wen, 9, kept the tally. She is my quietest camper. Her tally sheet was the neatest thing made all week.

Target of 15 decks: met, with 26. But look at the second row. Five of our own families ordered. Some were proud parents supporting their kids. That is kindness, not demand. The first row is the real signal. 16 of 22 families with a 4 to 7-year-old ordered. That's 73%.

A pre-order is still only a promise. On Friday, 24 of the 26 were collected and paid. The last 2 were collected the next week from the centre's front desk. So the promise held this time. See Friday's results.
