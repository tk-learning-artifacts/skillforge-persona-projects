Part 1

Market research report

Is there a real problem, who has it, and will they act? Twelve interviews, one national dataset and a demand test.

The students, parents, staff and their words are made up, like me. So are the counts from our demand test. The NEA waste figures are real.

01Where the idea came from

In week 1, the class listed 10 problems they meet at school. Then they voted.

  1. Canteen queues at first recess.
  2. Uniforms that stop fitting mid-year. (won the vote)
  3. Water bottles left in the hall.
  4. No shade at the bus stop outside school.
  5. Old textbooks thrown away each December.
  6. Wet umbrellas making the stairs slippery.
  7. Lockers that jam.
  8. Lost PE kit after CCA.
  9. Plastic cups at the drinks stall.
  10. Group work with nowhere quiet to meet.

Problems 2 and 8 merged. Our How Might We question:

How might we get outgrown uniforms to students who need the next size, without anyone feeling singled out?

The last part came from a student, Farhan. He said, "If it's only for poor kids, no one will go." That one line shaped everything after.

Thousand tonnes. In 2019 NEA's recycled and disposed figures add to 167, not 168, because of rounding.

What the class noticed:

  • In 2025, 231,000 tonnes of textile and leather waste were thrown out. Only 6,000 tonnes were recycled. That is 3%.
  • The rate has stayed at 4% or below since 2019. In 2015 it was 8%.
  • Food waste, by comparison, was recycled at 18% in 2025. Plastics were at 4%.
  • Semakau Landfill will run out of space by around 2035, at current rates (MSE).

"Textile and leather" covers far more than uniforms: clothes, shoes, bags, curtains. Our swap is tiny next to these numbers. I said that out loud. A student asked, "Then why bother?" We spent 20 minutes on that question. It was the best lesson of the term.

03How we interviewed

  • Weeks 1 and 2. Twelve interviews in total.
  • Students interviewed 6 schoolmates at recess, in pairs. One asked, one wrote. First names only.
  • I interviewed 4 parents at a Parent Support Group meeting, with the school's agreement.
  • I also talked to the uniform shop owner and a general office staff member.
  • Nobody pitched the idea until the last question. We asked about the last time, not the future.

Our question bank

  1. When did you last need a uniform item? What was it?
  2. What did you do? Where did you get it, and how long did it take?
  3. What happened to the last item you outgrew?
  4. What was the hardest part?
  5. Students only, asked last: would you pick one up at a table during recess?

Question 5 is about the future, so it is weak. We kept it, asked it last, and treated the answers as a hint only.

04Interview notes

1. Aisyah

Sec 1 student

"My PE shorts fit in January. By June they were too short."

Her mum bought new ones at the shop in Term 2. Recess table? "Maybe, if no one's there."

PE kit is outgrown first.

2. Ravi

Sec 2 student

"I wore tight pants for a whole term."

He did not want to ask his parents to pay again. Recess table? "No. People would know."

Some students hide the problem to save their family money.

3. Mei

Sec 4 student

"My mum keeps three old skirts for no one."

She would give them away today. Recess table? "Yes, why not."

Supply is sitting in cupboards at home.

4. Jun Hao

Sec 1 student

"I'd never take one at recess. My friends would see."

He wears his cousin's old shirts. Nobody at school knows.

Being seen is the barrier, not second-hand itself.

5. Nisha

Sec 3 student

"I lost my PE shirt at CCA and needed one by Thursday."

She bought a new one at full price that week. Recess table? "Not with my CCA friends around."

Lost kit needs a fast answer, too.

6. Daniel

Sec 2 student

"I don't care if it's used. I care if it's fast."

His pants tore before a uniform check. Recess table? "Sure."

Speed matters more than newness for some.

7. Mrs Tan

Parent of a Sec 1 boy

"I bought three sets in December. By May one shirt was tight."

She went to the uniform shop on a Saturday. It was closed.

The shop's hours do not fit every parent.

8. Mr Lim

Parent of three

"We don't qualify for school help. Three of everything, every year."

His youngest gets hand-me-downs. The eldest always needs new.

Cost bites hardest just above the help line.

9. Mdm Siti

Parent of a Sec 3 girl

"I'd give, not buy. Buying used feels like taking from someone poorer."

She donated two bags of clothes last year, to a charity drive.

Our sign must say the swap is for everyone.

10. Mrs Kaur

Parent of a Sec 2 girl

"The school gave us uniforms in January. By July her shoes were done."

Her family is on MOE FAS. New shoes cost her more than anything else that term.

FAS covers the start of the year. Shoes are a need we won't meet, for hygiene.

11. Mr Koh

Uniform shop owner

"Used is fine. Just don't sell near-new ones outside my door in December."

December and January are his busiest months. Mid-year he keeps few large sizes. He offered to tell parents about the swap when a size runs out.

He can be a partner, not a rival.

12. Ms Ong

General office

"Every year end we have a box of PE shirts nobody claims."

About 40 a year. Last year's 22 were still in the store room.

Free stock, if the school agrees.

05What we found

  • Timing beats price. 8 of 12 talked about a size or timing problem. Only 3 raised cost on their own: Ravi, Mr Lim and Mrs Kaur.
  • PE kit goes first. It is outgrown, worn out and lost more than anything else.
  • Being seen matters. 4 of 6 students would not pick up at a recess table.
  • The stock exists. It sits in cupboards at home and in the lost-and-found box.
  • The gap is mid-year. Families on MOE FAS can get free attire on a need basis. Families just above the line, and anyone who grows in June, have no help.

06Demand test: request slips

Interviews show a problem. They do not show that people will act. So in weeks 4 and 5 we ran a request slip.

  • Every form class got slips. A student wrote first name, class, item and size.
  • Form teachers sent the slips to us in a sealed envelope. Only the team leader and I saw names.
  • A donation box stood at the general office. Families dropped off washed items.
  • We set our targets in week 3, before any slip went out. Writing the number first stops you from moving the goalposts.

Of the 88 items, 17 failed our check. 9 were stained, 5 torn and 3 from another school. Ms Ong's 22 lost-and-found PE shirts all passed after a wash.

Look at the size table. Requests lean large, and stock leans small. Students who grow need the next size up. Students who give have outgrown the smaller one. I missed this until swap week. It was there in week 5.

Is this proof of demand? It is proof that people want free items in the right size. A slip costs nothing to fill in. Swap week, in part 2, is stronger proof: people came and took items home. Neither proves people will pay. The plan deals with that.
