Unit plan · Sec 1 Science with computer science

Sort it like a computer

Students write a yes/no key to sort everyday objects by their properties. Then they teach the key to a Scratch bot. A key is an algorithm, and this unit makes that visible.

  • Sec 1 G2 and G3 Science
  • 6 classes of about 36
  • 2 lessons of 60 minutes
  • 3 trainers, with each class's science teacher

I'm Priya. I plan courses for a team of trainers. A school asked us for one computer science unit inside a subject. It was for every Sec 1 class. I wrote it once so that three trainers could teach it the same way. This page has the plan, the Scratch project, student work and what I learnt.

Why Science, and why a key

Look at the Sec 1 G2 and G3 Science syllabus. One section is "Exploring Diversity of Matter by its Physical Properties". Two of its outcomes are why I chose it:

  • "classify a number of common everyday objects and recognise that there are many ways of classifying the same group of objects"
  • "communicate findings on classification and justify reasons"

Science teachers already use keys for this. We did not add a topic. We added a way of looking at a key.

The CS idea

An algorithm is a list of steps. Anyone who follows it exactly gets the same result. A yes/no key is one.

  • Selection Each step asks one yes/no question. The answer decides which step comes next. In Scratch, that is an if-else block.
  • Precision "Is it heavy?" fails, because two people can answer it differently. "Does it float in water?" works, because you can test it.
  • Abstraction Each question uses one property and ignores the rest, like colour or size.
  • Decomposition "Which object is this?" is hard. Four small questions are easy.
  • Testing Groups swap keys. If a stranger can't follow yours, it is a bug, not their mistake.

One link to binary is worth a minute with fast groups. Each yes/no question is one bit of choice. So 3 questions can split at most 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 objects.

For the team

Six classes and three trainers can mean six versions of one lesson. So we plan for it.

  • One briefing of 30 minutes the week before. Each trainer runs the swap test on the others.
  • The same trays, starter file and slides in every room.
  • One marking guide with three levels. It is in the student work page.
  • After the first two classes, we meet for 20 minutes. Each trainer brings three keys, and we agree their levels.
  • Science teachers keep the keys for their density lessons.

Next steps across SkillForge: "Coding with Scratch" goes deeper into Scratch. "Machine Learning and AI Basics" is a good contrast. Here, people write the rules of the sort. In machine learning, the computer finds rules from examples.

Reflection

Clear link to the subject

Strong. Every question in a key tests a physical property. The syllabus asks for exactly this sort. The best moment came from the science side. Two groups had different keys that both worked. That is the "many ways of classifying" outcome, shown on the board.

CS idea explained correctly

Mostly. "A key is an algorithm" landed well. "Selection" needed the Scratch lesson before it made sense. One limitation sits in the model key itself. "Can you wrap it round a pencil?" is about shape, not material. A student said a steel paper clip would wrap too. She was right. Our key sorts these 5 objects, not metals in general.

Works for all students

Partly. Lesson 1 worked in every class. Groups chose their own questions, and the question bank helped weaker readers. Lesson 2 was harder for G2 classes. Pairs who typed fast finished. Others coded two questions before the bell.

What I would change

I planned lesson 2 as if the lab would be ready at minute 0. It was not. In the first class, logging in and loading took 12 minutes. I had planned 8. Next time trainers pre-load the starter during the break before. I would also put the "or" block for "y or yes" into the starter. In that class, 7 of 18 pairs lost testing time to the "yes" bug. That is a fine lesson once, but not seven times.
