Session 2: Make it a Scratch game

Pairs turn last week's dot cards into a Scratch game. Bit the robot asks for a number, and you flip cards to make it.

  • WhoThe same 24 P4 pupils, in 12 pairs.
  • Time90 minutes, with a 5-minute break.
  • Devices12 school laptops, one per pair.
  • Scratchscratch.mit.edu with no log-in. Pupils save to the laptop.

Learning goal

Pupils build a game with a loop, an if and an input. They use it to practise binary from 0 to 31.

Success criteria, in pupil words

  1. All 5 of our cards flip and change the total by the right number.
  2. Bit asks for 5 numbers, using a repeat loop.
  3. Bit waits until our total matches, then says so.
  4. I can explain what the if-else block on a card does.

Materials

  • 12 laptops. I copy dot-cards-starter.sb3 onto each one the day before.
  • The finished game, dot-cards.sb3, on my laptop and the projector.
  • 12 step cards with Bit's script in colour. It is shown below.
  • Backup: 6 envelopes of paper Scratch blocks, for laptops that won't log in.
  • Last week's dot cards, one set per pair.

The starter file

Everything is drawn already. Only Card 16 has scripts. Pupils read them first, then copy them to the other cards.

Copying a script: drag it onto another sprite's thumbnail in the sprite list. Then change 16 to that card's number, in both places.

The plan, minute by minute

  1. 0 to 10 minArrive and log in to the laptops. Log-ins are slow, so pairs warm up with dot cards while they wait. One calls a number, the other shows it.
  2. 10 to 20 minI play the finished game on the projector. We break it into parts on the board. Cards flip, the total changes, Bit asks, Bit checks. Those are our jobs for today.
  3. 20 to 35 minOpen Scratch. File, then "Load from your computer", then the starter. Read Card 16's script together. "What happens if I click it twice?" Pairs copy the scripts to Cards 8, 4, 2 and 1. They change the numbers.
  4. 35 to 40 minTest. Click 8 and 2: does the total say 10? A wrong total means a number was not changed. Pairs fix their own.
  5. 40 to 45 minBreak, away from the screens.
  6. 45 to 70 minBuild Bit's game from the step card. Drivers swap every 10 minutes when I ring the bell. I walk round with three questions: "Where is your loop? Where is your if? Where does the player give an input?"
  7. 70 to 80 minSwap seats with the next pair and play their game. Done early? Add Bit's last question with "ask and wait".
  8. 80 to 87 minTwo pairs show their game. Everyone answers the 2 check questions with a thumbs up or down. Then they explain to a partner.
  9. 87 to 90 minFile, then "Save to your computer". Shut down.

Check: 10 + 10 + 15 + 5 + 5 + 25 + 10 + 7 + 3 = 90 minutes.

Bit's step card

This is the whole script. On the printed card, the blocks for steps 1 to 4 are numbered. The last question is the extension.

  1. Set score and total to 0, and broadcast reset so all cards turn face down.
  2. Repeat 5: pick a random number from 1 to 31 and say it.
  3. Wait until total = target. That is the check.
  4. Add 1 to the score, say "Yes!", then reset the cards.
  5. Extension: ask a question, then use if-else on the answer.

Check questions

  1. Card 16 has dots up, and you click it. Which part of the if-else runs? (The top part. It turns face down and takes 16 off the total.)
  2. What would change if the repeat said 10 instead of 5? (Bit would ask for 10 numbers.)

For every pupil

  • Pairs who struggle: skip building Bit. Open the finished game and change the numbers instead. Make Bit ask from 1 to 15, and play with 4 cards.
  • Pairs who race ahead: add a Card 32. Change pick random to 1 to 63. Then count the flips and make Bit say how many you used.
  • Few devices: pairs share, and the driver swaps every 10 minutes.
  • Laptops that won't log in: the pair builds Bit's script with paper blocks. They join a laptop pair for testing after the break.
  • Pupils who read slowly: the step card uses block colours. They match colour first, then words.
