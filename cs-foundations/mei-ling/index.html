Flip to 31

A 2-session computer science mini-unit for my P4 coding club. Pupils learn binary with dot cards, then build a dot-card game in Scratch. It links to P4 maths: place value and number patterns.

I'm Mei Ling. I run an after-school coding club for upper primary pupils. My club has no subject of its own, so I borrowed one. Our P4s learn place value in maths this year. Binary is place value too, with a 2 where they expect a 10.

The CS idea, and the maths link

A bit is one thing with two states. Here it is a card, with its dots up or down. Five cards are five bits. They make 32 patterns, so you can show every number from 0 to 31.

Inside a computer there are no tiny 1s and 0s. A bit is a voltage that is high or low. We write 1 and 0 because it is quick. I say this out loud in session 1. I have said the wrong version before.

Each place is worth 2 times the place on its right. Each place holds 0 or 1. 8 + 4 + 1 = 13.

Two more links come up on their own. Every odd number has the 1 card up, which pupils met in P2. Counting from 0 to 31 makes a pattern too. The 1 card flips every time. The 2 card flips every second time, and the 4 card every fourth time.

The two lesson plans

Session 1: Dot cards, no laptops

Pupils find the card pattern and turn numbers into binary with an algorithm. Then they count to 31 and link it to place value. Printable cards and a record sheet are on the page.

Session 2: Make it a Scratch game

Pairs load a starter file and copy one card's script to the other four cards. Then they build Bit's game with a loop, an if and an input. There is a paper plan for when the laptops won't log in.

Play the Scratch project

Click the green flag. Bit asks for 5 numbers. Click cards to flip them until the total matches. Then Bit asks one last question: type your answer and press the tick.

The blocks are the real scripts. The block that each script is on lights up yellow as it runs. Watch the "wait until" block glow while Bit waits for you.

On a keyboard, press Tab to reach a card and Enter to flip it. This player runs on this page. It copies how Scratch runs blocks. It may differ from Scratch in small ways, like timing.

My reflection

Clear link to my subject

The link to place value did more than I planned. Pupil C wrote "821" for cards 8, 2 and 1. She was using tens and ones. That mistake opened the best 5 minutes of session 1. We put her answer next to the right one. Then we talked about what each place is worth.

CS idea explained correctly

I simplify, and I know it. In my starter last year, I said "computers only understand 1 and 0". A pupil asked if there are tiny zeros inside. Now the plan says it plainly. A bit is anything with two states. In a chip, it is a voltage.

Works for all students

Session 1 needs no devices, so it always runs. The 4-card set helps pupils who struggle, and the sixth card keeps fast pupils busy. In session 2, pairs share the 12 laptops. The paper blocks kept one pair going when their laptop would not log in.

What I would change

Session 2 is too full. Only half the pairs reached the last question before pack-up. Next time I would give Bit's script as printed blocks to put in order first. Also, the game only checks the total. A pupil can click at random until it matches. I would count the flips and have Bit say how few it could take.
