Session 1: Dot cards, no laptops

An unplugged binary lesson for P4. Pupils count with 5 dot cards and link them to place value.

  • Who24 P4 pupils. 6 groups of 4, in pairs for card work.
  • Time90 minutes, with a 5-minute break.
  • RoomOur club classroom. Desks in 6 islands.
  • DevicesNone.

Learning goal

By the end, pupils can show any number from 0 to 31 with dot cards. They can say why each card is double the one on its right.

Success criteria, in pupil words

  1. I can make a number my partner calls out, starting from the biggest card.
  2. I can read cards that are up and say the number.
  3. I can say how dot cards are like tens and ones, and how they differ.

Materials

  • 1 big set of 5 dot cards, printed on A4 card, for the front.
  • 12 small sets of 5 cards, one per pair, each in a zip bag. Print them below.
  • 24 copies of the count-to-31 record sheet, also below.
  • 24 exit ticket slips with the 2 check questions.
  • Whiteboard and markers.
  • I cut the cards before club. Pupils do not use scissors today.

The plan, minute by minute

  1. 0 to 5 minArrive, attendance, water bottles. Bags under the desks. I tell them: "No laptops today. Next week you build this in Scratch."
  2. 5 to 15 minStarter. Five volunteers stand at the front, each holding one big card face down. I turn over the 1, 2, 4 and 8 cards in order. The class guesses the next card. Then: what comes after 16? (32, then 64.) Ask: "What is the rule?" Each card is double the last.
  3. 15 to 30 minMake a number. I show 9 with the big cards, starting from the biggest. I say the algorithm out loud: "Does 16 fit in 9? No, keep it down. Does 8 fit? Yes, flip it up. 1 left. Does 4 fit?" I write it as 3 steps on the board. Pairs then make 20, 15 and 8 with their small cards. Partners check each other.
  4. 30 to 45 minCount from 0 to 31. Each pair flips their cards to count up, one number at a time. One partner flips, one fills in the record sheet. Ask: "Which card flips every time? Which flips every second time?"
  5. 45 to 50 minBreak. Cards stay on the desks, face down.
  6. 50 to 65 minPlace value. On the board, I write 13 in a tens and ones chart. Then 13 in dot cards: 01101. Ask: "In our numbers, each place is how many times the next?" (10.) "On the cards?" (2.) Then: "Look at your sheet. What is true of every odd number?" The 1 card is up. I tell them what a bit really is: anything with two states. In a computer, it is a high or low voltage.
  7. 65 to 80 minNumber of the day. Pairs show today's date, their age and the number of pupils in the club. Then a speed round. I call a number, and pairs race to hold it up in cards. Fast pairs get a sixth card to work out on paper.
  8. 80 to 87 minExit ticket. Each pupil answers the 2 check questions alone on a slip.
  9. 87 to 90 minPack up. Cards back in the bag in order: 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.

Check: 5 + 10 + 15 + 15 + 5 + 15 + 15 + 7 + 3 = 90 minutes.

Check questions

  1. The 8, 2 and 1 cards are up. What number is that? (11.)
  2. Show 19. Which cards are up? (16, 2 and 1. In binary, 10011.)

I read the slips that evening. A wrong answer to question 1 tells me about reading cards. A wrong answer to question 2 tells me about the algorithm. See three sample tickets.

For every pupil

  • Pupils who struggle: start with 4 cards, 8, 4, 2 and 1. Numbers stop at 15. Their partner reads the algorithm out loud from the board.
  • Pupils who race ahead: what is the next card? How high can 6 cards go? (63.) What about 8 cards? (255.)
  • Pupils who find reading hard: the record sheet has a dot picture above each column.
  • Pupils who won't speak up: the speed round is in pairs, so nobody answers alone.
  • Noise: 12 pairs racing gets loud. I tell the class next door first.

Print the cards and the record sheet

One set per pair: print on card, 1 page per set. The record sheet goes on plain paper.
