A micro:bit V2 caseThe Volume Box

A wall case for the noise meter in my P6 makerspace. It holds a micro:bit and its battery pack. The lid presses on, with no screws and no glue.

I'm Aisha. I teach design and making in a school makerspace. Our noise meter is a micro:bit that shows a bar graph of the room's sound. It used to sit on a shelf with the battery pack hanging off it. It fell off twice in one term. So the brief, "an enclosure or mount for a classroom device", had an easy answer.

Short rationale

I started with what pupils will touch. They press buttons A and B and swap the batteries. They read the LEDs from across the room. Everything else came from that.

  • The front is one big window. The LEDs, buttons and logo stay open to fingers.
  • The battery pack gets its own bay. Its switch shows through a second window. Nobody opens the case to turn it off.
  • The lid presses on. A P6 pupil can open it with a thumbnail. There are no tiny screws to lose.
  • Two parts, both flat on the bed. No supports to snap off, so no craft knives at the printer.
  • A keyhole on the back hangs it on a pinboard pin, at eye level.
  • It is plain on purpose. Pupils decorate the lid with stickers, or remix the lid in Tinkercad.

The board gets a loose gap. Pupils lift it out to plug in USB, so it must drop in and out. The lid gets almost no gap, because it must grip. That number came from my printer, not from a guide.

The tolerance test result below is from my print log. Like me, the log is made up.

Sticky label on our printer

Tolerance test, PLA, 0.2 mm layers.

Snug: the 10.0 mm hole. Sliding: 10.2 mm.

So snug is 0 per side here. The guide says 0.1.

My printer runs a little loose. A 0 mm gap felt risky, so the lid uses 0.05 mm per side. That is half a step on the test plate. Your printer will differ. That is why the lid maker is on this page.

The lid holds both parts still. Its lip rests on the battery pack. Its four posts stop 0.2 mm above the board. The board can't rattle or be crushed.

Printability

  • The body prints back-down. Every wall goes straight up, so it needs no supports.
  • The lid prints face-down, lip pointing up. The windows are just gaps in the first layers.
  • Nothing hangs over thin air. The keyhole and the lead notch go straight through.
  • Walls are 2.5 mm. With a 0.4 mm nozzle, that is solid plastic, with no thin gaps to fill.
  • Corners are square, so bed grip matters. A skirt is enough on a clean bed. Add a brim if the corners lift.

Before any print, my pupils check three things in Cura's preview: the first layer is whole, no supports appear, and the time is under our cap.

Strength vs time

This case carries no load. It must survive a fall from a desk. The lid must come off a hundred times. So I spent time where it shows, and saved it everywhere else.

These print times and test results are from my print log. Like me and my class, the log is made up. The settings are real Cura settings. The sums use the planning rule from SkillForge's printing article.

Body settingsTimePlasticWhat I saw
0.28 mm layers, 2 walls, 10% infill1 h 45 min26 gRough lip edge. The lid gripped on one side only.
0.2 mm layers, 3 walls, 15% gyroid2 h 35 min31 gEven grip. Survived 5 drops from desk height.
0.12 mm layers, 3 walls, 15% gyroid4 h 10 min31 gSmoother. Nobody could tell from 2 m away.
0.2 mm layers, 3 walls, 40% infill2 h 55 min37 gNo difference I could feel.

The 40% row matches Prusa's advice: walls set most of a part's strength, more than infill. The lid prints in 38 minutes and uses 8 g.

The class sum

My P6 class of 36 works in 9 groups of 4. Each group gets one case. SkillForge's planning rule adds 5 minutes per print for heating and clearing.

  • Bodies: (155 + 5) × 9 ÷ 60 = 24 hours.
  • Lids, 4 to a plate: (152 + 5) × 3 ÷ 60 is about 8 hours.
  • Total: about 32 printer hours. I am in the makerspace about 7 hours a day. So that is about 5 school days.

The printer only runs while I am in the room. At 0.12 mm, the bodies alone would need about 38 hours. That is the second week I don't have.

Three versions

Version 1 and 2 are real files in the 3D view above. The test notes, like me, are made up.

Version 1

  • The lid used the guide's sliding gap, 0.2 mm per side. On the wall it dropped off when a door slammed.
  • The board sat on a solid block. The battery plug on its back hit the block. The board stood 5 mm proud. The lid would not close.
  • The battery window was 44 mm wide. The switch sat behind the frame.

Version 2

  • I printed the tolerance test, then drew the lid at 0.05 mm per side. It grips.
  • The block got a 7 mm hollow under the board.
  • The battery window grew to 56 mm. Pupils can reach the switch with the lid on.
  • New problem: it hung on Blu Tack, and fell on day 3.

Version 3, the final case

  • A keyhole on the back: an 8 mm round hole with a 4 mm slot above it. It hangs on a pin or a small hook.
  • The pin head sits in the hollow under the board. The case lies flat on the wall.

What I would change

The case closes off the edge connector. Next term I want to add a sound sensor or a light on crocodile clips. Right now, pupils must take the lid off to clip anything on. I would add a slot along the bottom edge for the clips. The lid also grips by friction only. On a printer that runs tight, my 0.05 mm lid will jam. A snap clip would forgive a wider range of printers.
