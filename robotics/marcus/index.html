Tape Runner

A micro:bit robot with two light sensors that follows a black tape loop on its own. Four corners and an S-bend, three laps, no hands.

I'm Marcus. I coach a Sec 3 robotics team for competitions. The brief was one autonomous behaviour, plus notes on how we tuned it. We picked line following, because it is the first thing judges ask for. It also shows tuning better than anything else. One number out, and the robot leaves the track.

My rule for the team: change one number per run, and log it. This page is that log, cleaned up.

My team, our CCA room and our hall are made up, like me. The run results on this page come from the simulator below, which runs the same rules as our code. Press the same buttons and you get the same numbers.

    Filming your own robot's demo

    For the real robot, we film one take with a phone on a stand. Nobody's face is in the shot, only the floor and the robot. The shot list:

    1. 5 seconds on the wiring. A student tugs each plug to show it holds.
    2. The robot on the start line. Press A. Show the tick after the sweep.
    3. Three laps with no cut. A stopwatch app in the corner of the frame.
    4. Press B. The robot stops. It scrolls the threshold. Read it out.

    One take, no edits. If you cut the video, judges assume you hid a crash.

    05Reflection

    The big win was calibration. Versions 1 and 2 used one fixed threshold. In the hall, by the windows, the tape read about 460. Our thresholds were 512, then 543. To the robot, the tape had vanished. It drifted off before it even reached the first corner. A 1.6-second sweep at the start fixed every room we tried.

    The surprise was the turn size. Halving it made the robot smooth, and I was pleased. Then the test showed 4 runs in 20 sliding off, three of them at the S-bend. Smooth is not the same as reliable. We had to go back up to a middle value, then raise both numbers together for speed.

    Testing under pressure helped. Twenty runs feels slow, but one good lap proves nothing. Version 2 looked perfect for its first 8 runs.

    What still fails, and what I would change

    • One run in 60 still left the track, at corner 4 in the hall. Our speed is close to the edge. At a real competition I would drop BASE by 50 and take the safer lap.
    • Two sensors can't tell "centred" from "lost". Once both sensors miss the tape, the robot drives straight on. A third sensor in the middle would fix that.
    • The calibration fails if you start the robot off the tape. It shows a cross, but a new student won't know why. I would scroll "PUT ME ON THE LINE" instead.
    • This is on-off steering. The robot only knows left, right or straight. Proportional steering, where the turn grows with the error, would be smoother at speed. That is the next unit.

    06Sources

    • Sensor: Vishay TCRT5000 datasheet. It has an infrared emitter, a daylight blocking filter and a peak operating distance of 2.5 mm.
    • Motor driver: Texas Instruments DRV8833, a dual H-bridge that runs from 2.7 to 10.8 V.
    • Pins and PWM: micro:bit edge connector. PWM can be active on only 3 pins at once.
    • Power: micro:bit power supply. Power small motors from outside the board.
    • MicroPython: micro:bit MicroPython pins. read_analog gives 0 to 1023.
