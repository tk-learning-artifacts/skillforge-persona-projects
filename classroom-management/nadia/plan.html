Many ways in: my management plan

Nadia Hussain. P5 class of 30, form classroom, micro:bit unit. Lessons are 60 minutes. Pairs share one laptop and one micro:bit.

1. What I believe

Most behaviour in my room is a pupil telling me something. It's too hard, too loud or too long. So I fix the room and the task first, and the pupil last. I plan for the range from the start, using Universal Design for Learning (UDL): many ways to engage, to take in information and to show learning. I use PBIS tiers for support. I teach expectations to everyone and give extra support to some. For the few who need more, I plan with others. Every routine has three forms: a picture card, a short phrase, and a sound or a gesture.

2. Three class promises

  • Look after each other. Kind words. Wait your turn at the laptop.
  • Look after the kit. Two hands for trays. Batteries stay in their packs.
  • Keep trying. Asking for help or a break counts as trying.

Each promise has a symbol on the poster. We act out "looks like, sounds like" for one promise a lesson in Week 1.

3. Routines

Coming in

I greet each pupil at the door. The Now and Next board is on the screen. Pupils sit, put their desk card on green, and read the board with their partner. I read it aloud once for everyone.

Kit out and kit back

There are 15 numbered trays, one per pair. Each tray holds a micro:bit, a USB cable and a battery pack with two AAA batteries. The kit monitor from each table collects two trays. At pack-up, the battery pack is unplugged first, and I count all 15 trays back. Batteries never come out of the pack in class.

Getting help: desk cards and stuck steps

Each pair has three desk cards: a green circle for "working", an orange square for "help" and a blue triangle for "break". Before the orange square, pupils try the stuck steps: read the picture step card, ask your partner, look at the hint wall. Then they turn the card and start the next step. I go to orange squares in order.

Changes and transitions

The Now and Next board shows the time left. At 2 minutes, the board shows a warning and I hold up two fingers. At the end, a soft chime plays and I say "Freeze". No change happens without a warning, and the board shows any change to the plan first.

Pairs and roles

I set the pairs and keep them for the unit. The Coder holds the mouse. The Checker reads the step card and tests the micro:bit. They swap when the board timer moves on. Role cards have a picture, so pupils don't need to read them.

Freeze: the emergency stop

For anything unsafe, I say "Freeze, hands up" and raise my hand. The board shows a big red stop sign. Pupils stop, lift both hands and look at me. Sound, gesture and screen all say the same thing, so every pupil gets it. We practise it in Week 1.

4. Breaks and the calm corner

Any pupil can turn their card to the blue triangle. I give a thumbs-up. If I haven't seen it within a minute, they go anyway. They turn the 5-minute sand timer and choose one tool. When the sand runs out, they come back and turn the card to green. I give a First, Then card with one small step. Two breaks in a lesson is fine. A third means a quiet check-in after class.

Calm-down kit

  • Two pairs of over-ear headphones, wiped every Friday.
  • A 5-minute sand timer, in a plastic case.
  • Two quiet squeeze balls. No clicking or popping fidgets: they distract the whole table.
  • A breathing card: a square to trace with a finger, breathing in and out along each side.
  • A feelings strip with five faces and words, to point at instead of talking.
  • The "back to work" card: turn your card to green, then look at your First, Then step.

5. Ways to show learning

At the end of each lesson, pupils pick one from the choice board. They can demo it, draw what the code does, tell their partner, or write three lines. All four count the same. The success criteria are the same too. Only the format changes.

6. When things go wrong

  1. Check the room first. Is it too loud, is the step too long, is the pupil stuck? Fix that.
  2. A quiet cue. I move closer, and point to the promise on the poster.
  3. A quiet word with a card. I crouch, use few words and show a First, Then card.
  4. An I-statement, in private: "When the tray is grabbed, I worry the micro:bit will break. Please use two hands."
  5. Extra support for some. Three times in two weeks means a daily check-in card with two targets. The pupil and I agree on the targets. This is like the PBIS Check In Check Out.
  6. Planning with others. I meet the SEN Officer, the form teacher and the parents together. We agree on one plan that every teacher uses.

No pupil misses break time or kit time as a punishment. Taking away the hands-on part usually makes the next lesson worse.

7. Tracking

I tally each break card and orange square on a class list, with the time. I add short A-B-C notes for anything bigger: what came before, what I saw, what happened after. Every Friday, I look for patterns, such as the same task, time or noise level. I share them with the SEN Officer each month. Records stay in the school's system, not on my phone.

8. A lesson with all of this in it

  1. 0 to 5 min. Greet at the door. Cards on green. Read the Now and Next board together.
  2. 5 to 12 min. I model today's step with the picture step cards. Pupils show a thumbs-up if ready.
  3. 12 to 14 min. Kit monitors collect trays.
  4. 14 to 32 min. Pairs code the step counter. Coder and Checker swap at 23 min.
  5. 32 to 36 min. Brain break: stand, stretch, and shake your micro:bit to count 20 steps.
  6. 36 to 50 min. Test and improve. Fast finishers take a challenge card.
  7. 50 to 55 min. Show your learning from the choice board.
  8. 55 to 60 min. Battery packs off, trays back and counted, laptops to the trolley.

Check: 5 + 7 + 2 + 18 + 4 + 14 + 5 + 5 = 60 minutes. I review the plan in Week 4 with the SEN Officer.
