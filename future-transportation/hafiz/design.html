Still on the road / Design

Design: specs and mockups

Two small detectors watch the crossing. If someone is still on the road when the green man ends, the cars wait. Never longer than 13 seconds.

3.1The idea in one picture

3.2Where the idea comes from

I did not invent this. The UK has used it for years in its Puffin crossings. The Department for Transport's guide, Traffic Advisory Leaflet 1/02, describes it:

  • On-crossing detectors are "normally mounted one on each side" and focused on the crossing area.
  • The clearance time after the green man "can be extended" while people are detected on the crossing.
  • It "can also give more time to slower moving pedestrians."
  • If the detector sees nothing, which may mean a fault, the controller gives the longest preset time.

Singapore poles already carry sensors. LTA has trialled crossings where a wave of the hand calls the green man. So a pole-mounted detector is not a strange new object here.

3.3Why not an AI camera?

Every vendor pitch I read says "AI". An AI camera could spot a walking stick or a wheelchair, and give time only to them. I don't think that is better.

  • I don't need to know who you are. I need to know if someone is still on the road. A presence detector answers that.
  • A camera that sees faces collects personal data. The PDPC expects a clear purpose and a plan for how long footage is kept. A detector with no images avoids most of that.
  • Picking who "deserves" time is the Green Man+ problem again. The mother with the pram, the man on crutches and the tired teenager are all still on the road.
  • A model trained elsewhere may do worse in heavy rain, glare or crowds. I would need local test data before trusting it. The simpler detector has a long track record.

If the trial shows the detector misses people, I would look at cameras again. With evidence, not because the word AI sounds modern.

3.4Specs

Sensor waits: specification
PartWhat it does
DetectorsTwo pole-mounted presence detectors, one per kerb, aimed across the road. No camera, no images. They must see people who are moving slowly or standing still.
ZoneThe road between the two crossing lines, kerb to kerb. The waiting area is left out.
Steady green manUnchanged: 6 to 10 s, as today.
Flashing green manUnchanged: 1 s per metre of road.
HoldAt the end of the flashing green man, if the zone is occupied, cars stay red. Check every 0.5 s. Release as soon as the zone is clear.
CapThe hold never goes past 13 s, the same as the largest Green Man+ extension. This limits delay to buses and cars.
Walkers at the kerbSee the red man during the hold, so nobody new starts.
Countdown timerWhere a crossing has a pedestrian countdown, it reaches 0 as now. The hold happens after it.
Green Man+Kept. A card tap still adds time at the start. The sensor covers whoever is still crossing after that.
Fault modeIf a detector fails its self-check, the controller gives the full 13 s every cycle and reports the fault. It fails on the safe side, as in the UK guide.
PowerFrom the existing signal pole. No new cable under the road.
Data keptPer cycle: the date, time, and seconds held. No images and nothing that identifies a person. Kept for the trial plus 12 months, then deleted.

3.5Timing rules, step by step

  1. Someone presses the button or taps a card. The green man comes on.
  2. Steady green man, then flashing green man, exactly as today.
  3. When the flashing ends, the walker signal turns to red man.
  4. The controller asks: is anyone in the zone? If not, cars get green. Done.
  5. If yes, cars stay red. Ask again every half second.
  6. When the zone is clear, or 13 seconds have passed, cars get green.
