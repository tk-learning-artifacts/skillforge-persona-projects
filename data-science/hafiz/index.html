Is the wettest day of the year getting wetter?

01 · QuestionWhere the question came from

In March 2026, NEA released its 2025 Annual Climate Assessment. It says annual rainfall rose by 92.5 mm per decade from 1980 to 2025. It also says Singapore got more rain on wet days, which points to heavier downpours.

That figure is an average of 32 stations across the island. My students can't download all 32 in one click. But NEA does publish monthly records for one station, Changi, on data.gov.sg. So I asked a smaller question that this data can answer.

My question

Has the wettest day of each year at Changi Climate Station got wetter from 1984 to 2025?

Why this is answerable:

  • "Wettest day" is a number in the data. NEA's file gives the highest daily total for every month. The biggest of the 12 is the year's wettest day.
  • One place and fixed years. No guessing what "Singapore" or "lately" means.
  • "Got wetter" can be tested. I look for a trend. I also compare the first 21 years with the last 21.

02 · AnswerMy answer, and how sure I am

No. At Changi, the wettest day of the year has not got steadily wetter since 1984.

  • The trend line slopes slightly down, by 6.5 mm per decade.
  • That slope is weak. I shuffled the years 10,000 times. 31% of shuffles gave a trend at least as steep. Luck makes slopes like this all the time.
  • The swings are huge. The wettest day ranged from 59.4 mm in 2022 to 216.2 mm in 2011.
  • The later half has a lower median, 92.3 mm against 129.9 mm. But the boxes overlap a lot, and both halves have years above 210 mm.

What this does not show. It does not show MSS is wrong. MSS averages 32 stations and counts a wet day differently. Changi is one station, in the east of the island. These files also only give whole days, not hourly bursts.

Why a box plot? A mean hides the spread. With rain, the spread is the story. The mean of all 42 years is 126.0 mm and the median is 127.5 mm. Those two agree, yet no single year looks "average".

05 · Spot the trickTry to fool yourself

Here is how a headline could say rain is "exploding". Move the start year to 2018. Then cut the axis at 50 mm. Same data, different story.

Wettest day of each year, from a start year you choose

The orange line is the trend for the years shown.

Trick card: From 2018 the trend is +135.2 mm per decade. From 1984 it is −6.5. Starting at a dry year makes any rise look huge. A cut axis makes it look worse.

Could the real trend be luck? Shuffle all 42 years 1,000 times. How often is the shuffled trend at least as steep as the real one?

Your share will change a little each run. The notebook uses 10,000 shuffles with a fixed seed and gets 31%.

06 · CleaningWhat I cleaned, in short

NEA's files were tidy, with no blanks and no repeats. The mess was in the details:

  • 2026 only has 9 months so far. I dropped it, or its total would look like a dry year.
  • I added up my yearly totals and checked them against SingStat's annual table. 1982 and 1983 did not match. SingStat says its data come from Changi only from 1984. So I start in 1984.
  • My 1991 to 2020 average came to 2113.3 mm. That matches the figure MSS publishes for Changi.

The 7 change notes and all the files are on the data page. Every step runs in the Colab notebook.

07 · Class projectThe 4-lesson unit

My Sec 3 class does the same thing in four 60-minute lessons. They start from a claim and ask a question one station can answer. They clean the data in Colab. Then they present a claim with one limit. The only data about students is an anonymous prediction poll. The unit plan has the privacy check, a chart checklist and a 4-point rubric.

08 · RubricHow I think it does against the rubric

  • Question is clear and answerable. Met. One station, one measure, fixed years. I narrowed it from the island-wide claim because that is all this data can answer.
  • Charts are fair and labelled. Met. Both start at zero, name the unit and the source, and have a table view. The trick chart is labelled as a trick.
  • Claims match the evidence. Met, I think. I say "no steady rise at Changi", not "rain is not getting heavier". The shuffle test stops me reading too much into a 6.5 mm slope.
  • Students' data is kept private. Met in the unit. My own analysis uses no personal data. The class poll collects no names or emails and is deleted after lesson 4.

09 · Next timeWhat I would change

One station was the right size for Sec 3. But it is a weak test of the MSS claim. MSS talks about heavier downpours. The best test would use hourly rainfall from many stations. NEA's monthly files can't show a one-hour storm that floods a road. Next time I would add a second station with daily data, such as Admiralty. Do the two agree?

I also used a straight-line trend. Rain comes in wet and dry runs, so a line is a blunt tool. I kept it because Sec 3 students can read it. The shuffle test checks it.
