How will I get to secondary school?

A data project with Census 2020 numbers, made for my P6 class after PSLE.

I'm Nadia. I teach learners with a wide range of needs. After PSLE, my P6 pupils start to worry about Sec 1. One worry comes up every year: "How will I get there?"

So I asked the data first. Then I planned a project where my class asks the same question about themselves. A pupil can read every chart as a chart, a table or plain sentences.

The question

How do primary pupils in Singapore get to school, and what changes in secondary school?

This question works for data because:

  • The census asked how each student usually travels to school. It sorts the answers by level, so primary and secondary sit side by side.
  • It is one year, 2020, and one group: resident students, meaning citizens and permanent residents.
  • The answer is a count, so it turns into a percent and a bar chart. My P6 pupils know both. Bar graphs start in P3. Percent comes in P5.

The numbers come from the Census of Population 2020 table on data.gov.sg. The census got its transport answers from a sample of about 150,000 households. So each number is an estimate for the whole country, not a head count.

One label needs care. The census says "No Transport Required". It does not say how those pupils get to school. The census report links it to schools being close to home. It does not say whether these pupils walk. So I call the group "No transport needed".

My answer

In 2020, about 4 in 10 primary pupils needed no transport. In secondary school, that fell to about 1 in 7. About 7 in 10 took public transport.

The evidence:

  • 41.3% of primary pupils needed no transport. For secondary pupils it was 14.4%.
  • 36.9% of secondary pupils took a public bus only. 32.6% took the MRT or LRT, or mixed a bus or train with another way. Together that is 69.5%.
  • The census report gives the middle travel time, the median. It was 15 minutes for pre-primary and primary pupils, and 30 minutes for secondary pupils.

So for many of my pupils, Sec 1 will likely mean a longer trip. Many will take a bus or train. That is a useful thing to know before the school year starts. Some pupils will want to practise the route.

What I can't claim

  • That my class will be the same. This is all of Singapore. My class is about 30 pupils from one area.
  • That it is still true in 2026. The census was taken in 2020.
  • That "No transport needed" means walking. The census does not say.
  • Why families choose a way of travel. The data counts choices. It does not explain them.

Choices I made for access

Fewer groups, labels on the bars, and stripes as well as colour. Each chart has three ways to read it.

  • I merged 11 census groups into 6. Tiny bars under 1% add reading load and tell a 12-year-old nothing.
  • Each group's name sits above its bar, in full. No legend to match up for chart 1.
  • In chart 2, secondary bars are striped as well as orange. Colour is never the only clue. That follows the WCAG rule on use of colour.
  • Every chart also comes as a table and as sentences. A pupil who can't read the chart yet can still join the talk.
  • I wrote "about 4 in 10" next to "41.3%". Both are true. Some pupils hold on to one more easily than the other.
  • The text size buttons and the short version are for pupils, and for tired teachers.

How I cleaned the data

I kept the raw file and merged the groups. Then I checked my numbers against the census report. All 8 change notes are on the data page.

The census table was neat, but not ready. It had 25 columns, split by boys and girls and by seven levels. One label had a comma inside quote marks. That breaks the file if you split it by hand. My sums matched two numbers in the census report exactly. A third was off by 0.1 points. I wrote that down instead of hiding it.

The spreadsheet page shows every formula. You can download the Google Sheets file and open it yourself.

The class project

Four 60-minute lessons. Pupils survey their own class on paper slips with no names. Then they compare with the census.

My pupils are 12, so they don't get online accounts for this. They answer two questions on a paper slip with pictures. I type the slips into the sheet and shred them. Small groups are merged before anyone sees the results. The lesson plan has the privacy check, a chart checklist and a 4-point rubric. Pupils choose how to show what they learned. It can be a poster, a talk or a chart.

How I think it does against the rubric

  • Question is clear and answerable. Met. One country, one year, two levels, one census question.
  • Charts are fair and labelled. Met. Bars start at zero and every bar is labelled. The source is named. Each chart has a table and a sentence view.
  • Claims match the evidence. Mostly met. I say "likely" about my own pupils, and I list what I can't claim. The 0.1-point gap is written down.
  • Students' data is kept private. Met in the plan: no names, no accounts, and slips shredded. Small groups are merged, and data is deleted after lesson 4.

What I would change

The census is from 2020, six years ago. Travel may look different now, and I can't check that with this data. I would also like travel time split by level. The census table on data.gov.sg mixes all levels together. So I had to use the report's medians.

One worry about my own plan: some pupils hate being asked about home at all. A paper slip with "prefer not to say" helps. But a pupil who lives far away may still feel watched. I keep the slips anonymous and I never ask who wrote what.
