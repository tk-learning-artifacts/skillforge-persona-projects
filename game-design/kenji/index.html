Prime Smash

Tap a prime to split the marked number. Split it all the way down before it lands.

    On a keyboard: keys 2, 3, 5 and 7, and 1 for 11. Left and right arrows change the target. P pauses.

    Rule sheet

    Goal

    Score as many points as you can before you lose 3 hearts.

    Rules

    1. Numbers fall in blocks. The block with the yellow outline and arrow is your target.
    2. Tap a prime. If it divides the number, the block shrinks to what is left. 84 split by 2 leaves 42.
    3. Keep splitting until nothing is left but 1. The block then pops.
    4. A prime that does not divide the number costs a heart. So does a block that reaches the ground.
    5. Tap another block to make it your target. Big numbers take longer, but score more.
    6. A popped block scores 10 points for each prime in it. 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 scores 40.
    7. Every 5 pops, the level goes up. Numbers get bigger and fall a little faster.

    Feedback

    • A right prime: a pop sound, sparks, and the block squashes.
    • The split so far shows under the block, like 84 = 2×2×…
    • A wrong prime: a buzz, a shake, and "84 ÷ 5 ✗" at the top.
    • Every sound also has a message in words under the game.
    • The end screen lists each number you split. It uses index form, like 84 = 2² × 3 × 7.

    Core loop

    Look at the number, pick a prime, see it shrink, pick again.

    Why winning needs the maths

    A quiz with a timer only rewards fast fingers. I wanted the maths to decide who wins.

    • Every wrong prime costs a heart. Guessing loses fast.
    • Points come from the number of primes. You need to see that 96 hides six of them.
    • You choose your target. Spotting that 77 needs only two splits is a real choice.
    • Some blocks are already prime, like 11. You must know it is prime to clear it in one tap.

    In version 1, wrong primes cost nothing. One student mashed every button and won. You can try it: that is the point of keeping version 1.

    Playtest notes from 2 rounds

    The students and their words below are made up, like me. The numbers come from the game's settings in each version.

    Round 1: version 1, four Sec 1 students

    I ran it at the end of an enrichment lesson. I stayed silent and wrote what I saw.

    • Note 1. Student A pressed 2, 3, 5, 7 over and over. He got the top score. He said, "I don't even look at the numbers."
    • Note 2. Blocks took about 9 seconds to land. Student B said 84 was gone before she could think.
    • Note 3. Student C cheered every time a block popped. The pop sound worked.
    • Note 4. Nobody used 11, because it was not there. Numbers like 77 never fell.

    Changes for version 2

    • From note 1: a wrong prime now costs a heart. Mashing no longer wins.
    • From note 2: blocks fall at half the speed, so they take about 19 seconds.
    • From note 4: I added the 11 button.
    • From note 3: I kept the pop, and added sparks and a squash.

    Round 2: version 2, four other Sec 1 students

    • Note 1. Students D and E lost all 3 hearts in under 40 seconds. Their first numbers were 126 and 150.
    • Note 2. Student F is red-green colour blind. He could not tell which block was his target.
    • Note 3. After the game I asked for one number they split. Nobody could tell me.
    • Note 4. The bell rang mid-game. Student G asked for a pause button.
    • Note 5. Student D pressed 2 on 150 and smiled when it shrank to 75. He was reading the numbers now.

    Changes for version 3, the final game

    • From note 1: level 1 uses only 2, 3 and 5. Numbers stay at 30 or under. Numbers grow every 5 pops. The first blocks take about 30 seconds to land. D and E were anxious, so I lowered the challenge.
    • From note 2: the target gets a thick yellow outline and an arrow. Colour is no longer the only clue.
    • From note 3: the split so far shows under each block. The end screen lists each number in index form.
    • From note 4: a Pause button, and the P key. The game also pauses if you switch tabs.
    • From note 5: nothing. That is the moment I wanted.

    Accessibility check

    I checked version 3 against 8 of the Basic guidelines from the Game Accessibility Guidelines.

    • "Include an option to adjust the game speed." Met in version 3: slow, normal or fast.
    • "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by a fixed colour alone." Fixed: outline and arrow.
    • "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by sounds alone." Met: every sound has words too.
    • "Ensure interactive elements / virtual controls are large and well spaced." Met: 54 px buttons with gaps.
    • "Ensure controls are as simple as possible, or provide a simpler alternative." Met: one tap per prime. The game picks the target.
    • "Provide high contrast between text/UI and background." Met: dark numbers on pale blocks.
    • "Use an easily readable default font size." Met: numbers are 24 px.
    • "Allow controls to be remapped / reconfigured." Not met. The keys are fixed.

    Build your own in MakeCode Arcade

    My students build their games in MakeCode Arcade. Its controller has direction buttons and an A button, not six prime keys. So their version works like this:

    1. Make a block sprite that falls slowly. Store its number in a variable.
    2. Make a variable for the chosen prime. Up and down change it through 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11.
    3. When A is pressed, check whether the number divided by the prime leaves no remainder.
    4. If so, divide the number and play a sound. Add the prime to a list.
    5. If it does not, take away a life and shake the sprite.
    6. When the number reaches 1, add points, show the list and make a new block.

    The unit plan walks students through this in 3 lessons.

    What I would change

    The game checks the maths, but not the method. A student can test 2, then 3, then 5 in order. With 3 hearts, that slow guessing still clears small numbers. A rule that scores more for no mistakes would help. Also, the keys can't be remapped yet. One student asked for the arrow keys on the left hand.
