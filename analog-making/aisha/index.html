Otter Dive

A cardboard box with a crank on the side. Turn it, and an otter rises slowly out of a wave. Then it dives in one snap. One snail cam does all of it.

I'm Aisha. I run the makerspace at my school. My Sec 1 classes make this in one 60-minute lesson, in pairs. They pick the creature and the cam. The otter is my demo. NParks believes smooth-coated otters came back to mainland Singapore in the 1990s. They mainly eat fish. So a diving otter is a story every pupil already knows.

Turn the crank

This is the build, running. It uses the real sizes from the template. The front wall is off, so you can see the cam. Pick a cam, then turn the crank. Drag the crank handle, use the arrow keys on it, or press the button.

Try this with your class. Turn the snail cam 3 full turns. Count the dives. Then turn it the other way and see what happens. Their cardboard version will travel a little less than the number here. Ask them why.

Build record

This site shows no photos. So the build is drawn, frame by frame, like a stop-motion film. The simulator above is the moving version. Sizes are in millimetres.

  1. The kit. I cut and score the strips before the lesson. Pupils never touch a craft knife.

  2. Fold on the dashed lines into a square tube. Tape the tab along its whole length.

  3. Poke an axle hole 35 mm up each wall. Push in 15 mm of straw. That is the bearing.

  4. Cut the cam from the template. Glue two layers so it grips the skewer.

  5. Push the skewer through a wall, the cam and the other wall. A tape flag each side stops the cam sliding.

  6. Glue the 30 mm foot to a flat coffee stirrer. Slide it up through the slot in the top.

  7. Tape the creature to the top of the stirrer. Glue the wave card to the front.

  8. Add the crank arm and a straw handle. Draw the "this way" arrow. Turn 3 times and measure the travel.

Try-out notes

My classes are made up, like me. The sizes, the cams and the fixes are real cardboard behaviour.

Version 1, with Sec 1 class A

  • Pupils cut their own frames from big sheets. The lesson ran to 72 minutes.
  • 5 of 16 cams slipped on the skewer. The crank turned. The cam did not.
  • The follower was a round skewer in a straw. Otters spun round and faced the wall.
  • 3 pairs turned the snail cam backwards. It jammed, and one cam tore.
  • Pupils measured 11 to 13 mm of travel. The simulator said 14.

What I changed for version 2

  • I pre-cut and pre-score every frame. That saved about 12 minutes.
  • Cams are two layers of card, with a tape flag either side. No more slipping.
  • The follower is now a flat coffee stirrer in a slot. A flat stick can't spin.
  • Every box gets a "this way" arrow by the crank. A snail cam only turns one way.
  • The travel gap is now a question, not a problem. The foot is card, and card squashes.

Version 2, with Sec 1 class B

  • 14 of 16 pairs had a working automaton by minute 45. The other 2 did by minute 50.
  • The glue gun station had a queue of 6 pairs at minute 22.
  • The stories got better once pupils picked their own cam. One pair made a hornbill that pecks, with the egg cam.

How it meets the rubric

SafetyI do all knife work before the lesson. Skewer tips are snipped flat. Hot glue stays at one station on a silicone mat, with a tap nearby. The guide says what to do for a burn.
Clear instructionsThe guide fits on one page. Each frame of the build record is one action. The template is true size, with every hole marked.
CreativityThe otter is only my demo. Pairs choose their own creature and pick the cam that fits its story.
Fits a 45 to 60 minute lessonThe plan is 60 minutes, pack-up included. Version 2 ran on time because I pre-cut the frames.

What I would change

One cam gives one movement. That limits the story. A diving otter is fine, but it can't wave or turn its head. Next term I'll add a second lesson with two cams on one axle. Travel is also small, about 14 mm. A short lever on top would double it, but that adds 10 minutes. And two glue guns for 16 pairs is not enough. I'd add a third, or switch to PVA and pegs for the cams.
