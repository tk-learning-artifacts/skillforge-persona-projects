$ Six Flags Before the Bell

A 60-minute capture-the-flag (CTF) lesson for Sec 3 Computing. Pairs solve six challenges in the browser, after a 5-minute talk on the law.

I'm Rajan. I wrote software for a living, and now I teach programming to secondary students. This is my end project for the Cybersecurity Basics module. The challenge board is the real thing. Play it as a student would.

# The six challenges

Each one is small, and each one is about how something really works. I wanted students to leave with six facts they can explain, not six tricks.

#ChallengePointsWhat it teaches
1Read the rules50Permission makes it legal
2Where does this link go?100Reading a hostname from the right
3Caesar's homework100Few keys means brute force wins
4Encoded is not encrypted150Base64 has no key
5The club page150The browser gets everything you send
6Build a passphrase200Length beats symbols

Five challenges attack something. The last one defends. I want the lesson to end on the defender's side.

# Why I built my own instead of using picoCTF

picoCTF is excellent, and my CCA uses it. For a whole class, three things pushed me to a board of my own.

  • Consent. picoCTF's terms say it is for students aged 13 or older. Students under 18 need a parent's or guardian's consent. That is fine, but collecting 28 forms takes two weeks.
  • Time. Account setup eats the first 15 minutes of a 60-minute lesson.
  • Fit. Many of picoCTF's General Skills problems ask you to use a Linux terminal. My Sec 3s have not used one yet. Every challenge here works in a browser tab.

Students who want more can go to picoCTF at home, with consent. The lesson plan says so.

# How it went

My class and its results are made up, like me. The challenges, flags and facts are real.

All 14 pairs got flags 1 and 3. Eleven pairs got 4 or more. Two pairs finished all six with 12 minutes left and wrote a challenge 7 for each other. The median score was 470 out of 750.

The talk landed better than I expected. The three cases did the work. A student argued that guessing a friend's password "on a dare" was fine. Another student answered him before I could: "A dare isn't a yes."

Three things went wrong.

  • Challenge 2 was too hard for where I put it. Most pairs picked link 1 and burned 10 minutes. I underrated how new "read a hostname from the right" is. They need a worked example first.
  • View source was blocked on six Chromebooks. My printouts saved challenge 5, but those pairs never saw the real page source. That was the point of it.
  • Two pairs started at challenge 6 and stalled. Defending is harder when you have not attacked yet. The order matters more than I told them.

# Against the rubric

  • Facts are correct. Every fact has a source on the page where it appears. The law figures come from the Act's text and a police news release.
  • Activities are safe and legal. Students attack only my site, which I open to them in writing. No accounts, no personal data. The links in challenge 2 can't be clicked, and the IP address reaches no real server.
  • Fits students' age and level. Mostly. Challenges 1, 3 and 4 suit Sec 3. Challenge 2 needs a worked example first, as I found out.
  • Students practise, not just listen. 30 of the 60 minutes are hands on keyboards. The talk is 5 minutes.
  • Reflection names changes. See the next section.

# What I would change

  1. Do one lookalike link together on the projector before the CTF. Then challenge 2 is practice, not a wall.
  2. Test view source on the actual school devices a week before, not the day before. Ask IT to allow it for the lab, or rebuild challenge 5 so the source is shown inside the page.
  3. Lock challenge 6 until a pair has two flags. Attack first, then defend.

One limitation I can't fix in a browser: the board checks flags on the device. A pair that reads my code could work some flags out without solving them. That is challenge 5's lesson again. A real CTF checks flags on a server. I chose no accounts and no server over a cheat-proof board, and the flag log is my check instead.
