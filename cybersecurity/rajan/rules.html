Ground rules for Sec 3 Computing. They apply in this lesson, in CCA and at home.

The six rules

  1. Attack only what you have permission to attack. Today that is the six challenges on this site and its files. Nothing else. Not the school network, not a classmate's laptop, not a real website.
  2. Permission comes from the owner. The school owns the school network. A friend can't give you permission for their parent's Wi-Fi. If you can't name the owner who said yes, you don't have permission.
  3. Never share, guess or use someone else's password or one-time code. That includes a friend who offers. If someone gives you their password, tell me. They need to change it.
  4. If you find a real weakness by accident, stop. Don't try it again to "make sure". Write down what you saw and tell me. I will pass it to the school's IT staff.
  5. Meaning well is not a defence. The law asks whether you had permission. It does not ask why you did it.
  6. Keep answers inside your pair until the bell. Help by asking questions, not by reading out flags.

What the law says

Singapore's Computer Misuse Act 1993 makes it an offence to get into a computer, program or data without permission. That is section 3.

  • A first conviction under section 3 can bring a fine of up to $5,000, up to 2 years in jail, or both.
  • A second conviction can bring up to $10,000, up to 3 years, or both.
  • If the access causes damage, the maximum goes higher.
  • Other sections cover changing data, blocking a service and sharing passwords or access codes for an unlawful purpose.

Practice sites like this one, or CyLab Security Academy (formerly picoCTF), are legal because their owners invite you to attack them. CyLab's terms say it is for students aged 13 or older, and students under 18 need a parent's or guardian's consent.

This is a summary for a class, not legal advice. The official text on Singapore Statutes Online is the one that counts.

Three cases to sort

Allowed, or not allowed? Say who would have to give permission.

  1. Your friend says, "My password is my cat's name. Bet you can't guess it." You guess it, log in, then log out at once.
  2. You read the code of the challenge board on this site to see how it checks flags.
  3. You notice the school printer's settings page opens with no password. You change nothing.
Answers
  1. Not allowed. A dare is not permission to log in. Only the account owner can let you in, and "bet you can't" is not that.
  2. Allowed. I own the site and I said yes, in writing, at the top of the board.
  3. Stop there. Opening a page you found by chance is not the problem. Going further would be. Tell me, and I tell the IT staff. That is rule 4.

If a student boasts about guessing a password

This is for teachers. It happens most terms.

  1. I stay calm and ask what they did, in private. Boasting usually means they don't know it was wrong.
  2. I point to rule 3 and the law. I don't threaten. I explain that permission is the whole difference.
  3. If a real account was used, the owner changes the password. I tell the school's IT staff and follow the school's discipline process.
  4. I give the student a legal place to practise, like the challenges here or CyLab if they are 13 or older and have consent.
