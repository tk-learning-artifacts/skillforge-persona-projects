Stop, Check, Tell

A 60-minute online safety lesson for a Primary 5 class. Pupils spot scam messages, learn three steps, and practise saying no out loud.

I'm Nadia. I teach pupils with a wide range of learning needs. This is my end project for the Cybersecurity Basics module. I planned it for one real kind of class: 30 P5 pupils, with some who read well below their age, some who find noise and surprises hard, and some who are ahead.

So every task has three ways in. You can listen, read or look at pictures. You can show what you learnt by saying it, drawing it or writing it. Nobody races a clock.

The three steps

Singapore's anti-scam campaign for adults is called "I can ACT against scams": Add, Check, Tell. Adding apps and settings is a job for parents. So for ten-year-olds I keep Check and Tell, and put Stop in front.

StopDon't tap, reply or send anything yet.
CheckLook for clues. Ask a safe adult if you are not sure.
TellTell a safe adult. Telling is never getting into trouble.

Adults can call the ScamShield Helpline on 1799, any time of day, to check if something is a scam. I tell pupils this so they can tell their parents.

The clue cards

Each clue has a picture and a word, the same every time. Pupils hold up a clue card when they spot one.

  • Hurry
  • Prize
  • Link or log in
  • Code or password
  • Secret
  • Money
  • Scary words
  • Who is it really?

Try it: the message sorter

This is the screen station from the lesson. Eight made-up messages, one at a time. Four are scams and four are safe. Every sender is made up.

Make it work for you:

This browser can't read aloud. A partner or adult can read the messages instead.

"Not sure: ask an adult" always counts as a safe choice. In real life, it is the right answer more often than "Scam".

How I built in access

  • Reading load. Every message has a "fewer words" version. The worksheets come in three versions, named by shape, never "easy" or "hard". Pupils pick their own.
  • Listening. "Read it to me" uses the voice built into the device. No headphones? A partner reads instead.
  • Colour. Colour is never the only clue. Each answer has a word and a picture too. I avoid red with green, and use a soft background, as the British Dyslexia Association's style guide suggests.
  • Type. Large sans serif text, left aligned, with no italics. Bold only for a few key words.
  • Pace. No timer, no sound effects and no class scoreboard. A pupil can go back to any message.
  • Showing learning. The exit task can be said to me, drawn or written.

How it went

My class and what happened are made up, like me. The messages are made up too. The facts and advice are real, with sources below.

The role-play was the best ten minutes. Pupils read a scam card to a partner, and the partner said "No. I will ask my mum first." Darren rarely speaks in class. He read the script card three times, then said it without the card.

The talk on the law worked better as a question than a rule. I asked, "If Jun Wei tells you his password, may you use it?" Most said yes, "because he told me". That is the exact idea the law is about, so we stayed on it.

Three things went wrong.

  • The Mum message. Eleven pupils called it a scam. "Scammers can pretend to be Mum." True, but I had made them suspicious of everything. They needed to learn that checking means calling back on a number you know, not refusing every message.
  • The Ah Ma voice note upset Farah. She lives with her grandmother. I should have warned the class first and offered a different message.
  • Read aloud. The device voice said "Ah Ma" badly, and two pupils with dyslexia asked me to read instead. A built-in voice is not the same as a familiar one.

Against the rubric

  • Facts are correct. The helpline, the campaign, the law and the advice on codes and voices are each linked to an official source.
  • Activities are safe and legal. No accounts and no personal data. Every message is made up. The fake messages pupils write stay on paper and in the room. The rules say so.
  • Fits students' age and level. Yes for most. The three worksheets and the fewer-words mode cover a wide reading range. The voice note needs more care for some children.
  • Students practise, not just listen. About 35 of the 60 minutes are sorting, writing and role-play. My talking is under 15 minutes in all.
  • Reflection names changes. See below.

What I would change

  1. Teach "check" before "scam". Start with a safe message and show what checking looks like: calling back on a known number.
  2. Warn the class before the voice note, and offer a swap. Any message about a family member can land close to home.
  3. Record my own voice reading each message for the screen station, instead of the device voice.

One limitation stays. Eight messages can't cover every scam. Real scams change fast, and pupils will meet kinds I did not show. The three steps are meant to work on messages they have never seen. I can't know from one lesson whether they will.
