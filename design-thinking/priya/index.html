The Two-Minute Handover

A phone screen a trainer fills in while pupils pack up. It tells the next trainer where the class really stopped and what will go wrong. My testers handed over with version 2 in under two minutes.

I'm Priya. I plan courses for a team of 12 trainers. We run a 10-week micro:bit programme after school. It is for P4 and P5 pupils in six primary schools. When a trainer is ill, another one covers the class. Those cover lessons were our weakest. So my users for this loop are adults: my own trainers.

1 Empathise

I thought I knew the problem. Cover trainers complained, so I assumed our lesson plans were too thin. I interviewed three trainers for 15 minutes each, by video call. I asked about the last cover lesson each of them gave or handed over.

"The plan said week 4, step counter. The class had never finished week 3. I found out 20 minutes in."Trainer A, covered a P5 class last month
"I do write a handover. On the MRT home. By then I've forgotten half of it."Trainer B, regular trainer
"Six micro:bits had flat batteries. Nobody told me. That's a whole pair-work lesson gone."Trainer C, covers two or three times a term
"Don't give me more to read. Tell me where to start."Trainer A, again

What they asked for

"A better lesson plan template." "More detail in the scheme of work."

What they needed

The state of the class: where it really stopped, and what will go wrong today. The plan only says where the class should be.

The second insight was about time. A regular trainer's only free moment is the two minutes while pupils pack up. Anything later gets written on the MRT, from memory, or not at all.

2 Define

I wrote one statement for each side of the handover.

The cover trainerA cover trainer needs to know where the class really stopped and what will trip them up, because the plan shows where the class should be, not where it is.

The regular trainerA regular trainer needs to hand over in the time it takes pupils to pack up, because after the lesson the details are gone.

My first draft was "Trainers need a better handover form." That hides a solution: a form. It was the one I already had in mind. So I reframed it.

How might weHow might we pass on the state of a class in the two minutes of pack-up?

3 Ideate

At a team meeting, five trainers and I wrote exactly 20 ideas in 15 minutes. Then we sorted them into four themes and dot-voted. The two picks are in bold.

Say it

  1. Voice note to the cover trainer
  2. Phone call the night before
  3. Ask the pupils where they stopped
  4. Teacher-in-charge passes it on
  5. Pupils record a 30-second recap

Paper

  1. A handover card in the kit box
  2. Sticky note on the printed plan
  3. A wall chart in the classroom
  4. Pupils tick steps in a booklet
  5. Tags on kit bags: green, amber, red

Screen

  1. A three-tap exit screen on the phone
  2. A shared slide per class
  3. A template in the team chat
  4. A long online form
  5. Photo of the whiteboard

Change the lesson

  1. Every lesson starts with a 5-minute recap
  2. Shorter lessons, so none run over
  3. Plans with a "catch-up" path
  4. Cover trainers shadow once a term
  5. Kit checked by the school, not us

Idea 6, the kit box card, travels with the kit, which is clever. But a cover trainer often gets the kit box only at the school gate. Idea 11 reaches them the night before. So I prototyped 11 first and kept 6 for the next loop.

The assumption to test

Trainers will fill in a handover during pack-up, if it takes under two minutes.

Idea 20 kept coming up. It's out of our hands, so it went to my manager, not into this loop.

4 Prototype

Version 1: a paper phone

I drew nine fields on a phone-sized card. It took me 40 minutes. No code yet. First I wanted to learn if anyone would fill it in. The paper version is in the test film. You can fill in a copy below.

  1. Class
  2. Date
  3. Lesson
  4. Where did you stop?
  5. Who was absent?
  6. Who is behind?
  7. Kit problems
  8. Behaviour notes
  9. Plan for next lesson

5 Test

I tested with three trainers. A and B had been interviewed. D had not been in the project. Each one taught a 10-minute slice of a lesson to colleagues. Then I said, "Pupils are packing up. Hand over." I timed them and stayed silent.

  • Trainer A, v1
    4:10
  • Trainer B, v1
    5:30
  • Trainer D, v1
    3:50

Bar length is time out of 6:40. Pack-up takes about 2 minutes, or 30% of that.

  • Note 1. Nobody finished in two minutes. All three stopped writing at least once to help a "pupil".
  • Note 2. "Where did you stop?" got "did the loop part" and "halfway". Trainer C read them and asked, "Halfway through which step?"
  • Note 3. Two trainers wrote pupils' full names under "Who is behind?". I had asked for that, without thinking.
  • Note 4. "Behaviour notes" and "Plan for next lesson" were left blank or said "ok".
  • Note 5. As the reader, trainer C looked at the first field and the kit field. She skipped the rest.

Then the team critiqued the paper version with "I like, I wish, What if".

I like the kit field. It's the one that saves a lesson.
I wish I could tap instead of write. My hands are full of micro:bits at the end.
What if it already knew the steps of the lesson, so I just pick one?

Version 2: three taps and a card

  • From note 2 and the What if: "Where did you stop?" is now a list of the lesson's steps. You tap the last one most pupils finished.
  • From note 3: no names anywhere. You give a number: how many pupils didn't finish. The cover trainer plans a catch-up group. Names stay with the school.
  • From note 4: behaviour and plan fields are gone. A one-line note is optional.
  • From the I like: kit problems are big tap targets. You add a number for missing or flat micro:bits.
  • From note 5: the cover trainer sees one card. The first line is "Start at step 5".
  • From note 1: a clock on the screen shows your time. Two minutes is the target.

Version 2 is a working web page, not a design picture. I built it in an afternoon. Then I ran the same test with the same three trainers, plus the same reader.

  • Trainer A, v2
    0:55
  • Trainer B, v2
    1:10
  • Trainer D, v2
    1:40

All three finished inside pack-up. Trainer C started each mock cover lesson at the right step. That was three out of three. Trainer D was slowest because he read every step in the list. He said, "I'd be faster by week 2."

    No pupil names

    Note 3 was the finding I was least proud of. My own form asked for names. Singapore has a Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Under it, personal data is data about a person who can be identified. A list of pupils who are "behind", sent around trainers' phones, is exactly that. A number does the job. The cover trainer plans a catch-up table for five pupils. Then they ask the class who is still on step 4.

    What this means for my team's year

    Version 2 only works because every lesson has a step list. That moves work to me. Our 10 weeks have about 8 steps each. That's about 80 steps to write and keep up to date. I'll add them to the scheme of work. Then the plan and the handover use the same numbers. A trainer then says "step 5" in a meeting, and everyone knows what it means.

    What I'd change

    Three trainers is a small test. In 2000, Jakob Nielsen wrote that 5 test users find about 85% of usability problems. With 3, I have certainly missed some. The tests were also mock pack-ups with colleagues. Real pupils are louder and ask more questions.

    The clock may push trainers to rush. Trainer D tapped a step without reading it in his first try. Next loop, I'd test the kit box card, idea 6, beside the phone. I'd also run version 2 in four real cover lessons.
