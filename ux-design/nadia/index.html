Tonight and Tomorrow

I redesigned one task on a school homework page. Pupils ask: "What is my homework tonight? What do I pack?" I designed it for P5 pupils who read slowly, lose their place or see poorly. Then I planned a lesson where my class does the same steps.

I'm Nadia. My classes hold a wide range of learning needs. When a page is hard to read, these pupils give up first. So they were my first users, not an afterthought. This is my end project for the UI/UX Design module.

1. The page I started with

The school's "Homework Board" is one long table. Every class and every week sits on one page. Pupils open it on a parent's phone at night.

I checked it against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics and the W3C's designing tips. Three problems stood out.

  1. Small pale text, all one size. Nothing tells you where tonight starts. That breaks "Aesthetic and Minimalist Design". It also fails the W3C tip "Provide sufficient contrast between foreground and background".
  2. "Due" is shown by red text only. That breaks the tip "Don't use color alone to convey information". About 1 in 12 men have a colour vision deficiency. That figure is from the US National Eye Institute.
  3. File icons with no words. You must remember what each icon means. That breaks "Recognition Rather than Recall".

2. Interviews

I interviewed 3 pupils and 1 parent, for about 10 minutes each. I asked about the last time they checked homework, not what they would like. Here are my notes, with the pain points marked.

Pupil 1, P5, has dyslexia

  • Last night she checked on her mum's phone, in bed.
  • "I scroll and scroll. Then I forget which row is today."
  • Pain point She reads every row from the top, every night.

Pupil 2, P5, has ADHD

  • He checks after dinner, then gets up to pack, then forgets.
  • "I did the homework but my workbook was at home." That happened twice last month.
  • Pain point Doing the work and packing for it are two separate memories.

Pupil 3, P5, low vision

  • She zooms in with two fingers. Then the table slides sideways off the screen.
  • "The red ones are late? Or is it the dark ones?"
  • Pain point She can't tell "due" from "not due".

Parent of a P4 and a P6 pupil

  • She shares one phone between two children at bedtime.
  • "I just want to see their class only, not the whole school."
  • Pain point Every class is mixed on one page.

One idea I would have got wrong without asking: I planned a weekly calendar. Nobody looked further than tomorrow. They wanted "tonight" and "tomorrow's bag", so that became the design.

4. Three wireframes

I sketched these in pencil first, then redrew them here. Boxes and labels only. One main action per screen. Three text sizes at most. No colour.

5. The accessible mock-up

I turned all three wireframes into one working page. It runs here in the browser. You can tick tasks and pack the bag. You can also switch between the tested and final versions.

Open the mock-up

The page also has a "Run the checks" button. It checks the page you are looking at:

  • Every colour pair, in light and dark mode. Normal text needs 4.5:1 and large text 3:1.
  • Tick box borders against 3:1, the rule for parts of controls.
  • Every input has a label, and every button has a name.
  • Every target is at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels.

The final version passes all of them. The tested version fails two kinds of check, on purpose. Its tick boxes have no name and are too small to tap. My testers ran into both.

I also added a "Text and spacing" panel. Pupils can pick bigger text, or wider spacing. Wide spacing uses the exact values in WCAG's text spacing rule.

6. Test notes from 5 pupils

I ran a think-aloud test with 5 P5 pupils on version 1 of the mock-up. Each used a school iPad alone, one at a time, for about 8 minutes. I gave 3 tasks and asked them to say their thoughts out loud. I stayed quiet and wrote.

  • Task A: "Find the Maths homework that is due tomorrow."
  • Task B: "You finished the English reading. Show that."
  • Task C: "Do you need your PE kit tomorrow?"

Every problem I saw

  1. Task A. Pupil A read "Due Thu" as "Due Tue". She said, "Oh, so it's late?" She has dyslexia.
  2. Task B. Pupil B ticked the English card. It vanished. "Where did it go? Did I delete it?" He searched for about 40 seconds.
  3. Task C. Pupil C scrolled past "Pack my bag" twice. It sat below the task cards. She took almost 2 minutes.
  4. Task B. Pupil D has low vision. She zoomed in. The tick box stayed small, and she missed it twice. "I can't hit the little box."
  5. Task B. Pupil E asked out loud, "Is the box the done one?" I stayed quiet. She guessed right.
  6. Task B. Pupils A and D both said the English steps were "a lot of words".

I ranked them two ways. Did it stop the task? How many pupils hit it?

The top 3 fixes, from the notes

  • From N1 "Due Thu" became "Due tomorrow, Thursday". Full day names, plus the word pupils actually say.
  • From N2 A ticked task now stays on the list, crossed out. It has an "Undo" button. That is "User Control and Freedom".
  • From N3 "Pack my bag" moved from the bottom to a tab at the top.

Two smaller fixes I also made

  • From N4 The tick button is now 48 px tall, with the word "Done". I added the "Text and spacing" panel too.
  • From N5 The word "Done" beside the tick answers her question.

Not fixed yet

  • N6 The English steps are shorter now, but teachers write the homework. I can't control their word count. See the limits below.

I tested once, with 5 pupils. Jakob Nielsen's 2000 article says 5 users find about 85% of problems. It also says to test again after you fix them. I have not run round 2 yet.

7. The lesson

My P5 class runs the same steps on their own idea. Each pupil designs a screen for something in school that confuses them. One 60-minute lesson covers the wireframe, a silent test and kind critique. Pupils interviewed two classmates the lesson before.

8. What I would change

The biggest gap is teachers' writing. My page can make text bigger and spacing wider. It cannot make a 60-word instruction short. Next I would give teachers a box that counts words and suggests three short steps.

My 5 testers were all strong enough readers to try the tasks alone. A pupil who cannot read yet would need the steps read aloud. I did not build or test that. I would also run a second round of 5 before calling it done.
