Box Box

An M5Stack on our robot reads the battery pack and sends it to an Adafruit IO dashboard. From the dashboard, the coach sends one word back. "BOX" turns the robot's screen red: come in, swap the pack.

I'm Marcus. I coach a secondary school robotics team. Last season our robot got slower in the third practice run of every session. The students blamed the code, then the wheels, then each other. It was the battery. This project is how we stopped guessing. It runs on the practice table, with my Sec 3 and Sec 4 team of four. It comes off the robot before any official run.

The brief: a mini telemetry demo. The device sends a sensor reading to the cloud and responds to a control message. Here the sensor reading is the pack's voltage. The control message is a pit call.

    03Data-flow diagram

    One reading goes down the left. The pit call comes back up the right. Each box says what the data looks like at that hop.

    What can fail at each hop

    • Hops 1 to 2: a loose pack plug. The reading drops towards 0 V. The screen shows it at once.
    • Hop 3: noise from the motors. That is why we average 10 readings.
    • Hop 4: the hotspot goes to sleep or walks away in a pocket. The board counts missed sends and keeps the screen live.
    • Hop 5: an expired certificate. The connect fails every time. The screen says "Retry".
    • Hop 6: too many data points. Adafruit IO rejects the publish and disconnects the board. That is why the board caps itself at 24 a minute.
    • Hop 7, coming back: a BOX sent while the board is offline is missed. On reconnect the board asks for the last pit call.

    04The code

    The full sketch, the secrets file and the dashboard setup are on the code page. The short version:

    • Arduino C++ on the M5Stack Core, with the M5Unified and Adafruit MQTT libraries.
    • One publish feed for the data, one for status notes, one subscribe feed for the pit call.
    • Four commands. Anything else is ignored.
    • Reconnects with a growing wait, from 5 s up to 60 s, and never gives up.
    • Keys live in secrets.h, which is in .gitignore.

    06Data clarity

    A pit wall is read in two seconds, under pressure. So every number needs its unit and its meaning.

    • Names. Feeds say what they hold: robot-volts, not feed1.
    • Units. Volts, always with V on the screen and the chart axis.
    • Precision. Two decimal places. A third would only show noise.
    • Smoothing. Each value is the average of 10 readings. It wobbles less, but reacts a little later. For a pack that drains over minutes, that trade is fine.
    • One line to watch. The chart has a dashed line at 6.6 V. Below it, while running, you swap. We picked 6.6 from our own runs, not from a datasheet.
    • Gaps are gaps. The chart breaks the line where readings are missing. It never draws a straight line across a dropout.
    • Freshness. "Last seen" turns amber after 15 s and red after 30 s. Old data looks old.
    • JSON. When students read the feed by HTTP, the value comes back as a string, "7.12". They convert it before comparing. The code page shows the reply.

    One thing to know about the number: a NiMH cell is rated 1.2 V, and Energizer rates capacity down to 1.0 V a cell. So a 6-cell pack is "empty" around 6 V at rest. Under motor load it reads lower than at rest. Compare runs under the same load.

    07Security basics

    • Encrypted link. MQTT over TLS on port 8883, with the certificate checked. Adafruit IO strongly recommends SSL on 8883 over plain 1883.
    • Key out of the code. The Adafruit IO key is in secrets.h, which is in .gitignore. It never goes on the projector or in the video.
    • Key leaks happen. If one does, regenerate the key on Adafruit IO. The old one stops working at once, so update the board.
    • Only 4 commands. Unknown text is ignored and never shown. Nobody can put a message on the robot.
    • No remote driving. A cloud message can change the screen and the beep. It cannot move a motor. A robot that moves on a network message is a safety risk at a crowded table.
    • Private dashboard. Only the coach's laptop is signed in. Students get the view, not the key.
    • Own network. A phone hotspot with a long WPA2 password, at practice only. Not the school Wi-Fi, unless IT has said yes.
    • Least data. Volts and a status note. No names, no locations.

    08Build it

    Parts

    • M5Stack Core (Basic): ESP32, 320 × 240 screen, three buttons, speaker.
    • A 4-wire Grove-style cable for Port B.
    • Resistors: one 20 kΩ and one 10 kΩ, 1% if you have them.
    • A small stripboard or breadboard, and a plug that matches your pack.
    • The robot's 6-cell NiMH pack, 7.2 V nominal.
    • A multimeter, a phone hotspot and a laptop with the Arduino IDE.

    Wiring

    Steps

    1. Unplug the pack from the robot. Wire the divider with no power on it.
    2. Check the resistors with the multimeter before soldering: 20 kΩ on top, 10 kΩ to minus.
    3. Plug in the pack. Measure the middle point with the multimeter. It must be under 3.1 V. Only then connect Port B.
    4. Make the three feeds and the dashboard. Fill in secrets.h.
    5. Upload the sketch. Compare the screen with the multimeter on the pack. Set CAL so they agree. Upload again.
    6. Run the test log above. Every trial, on the practice table, before you trust it.

    Safety

    • Never short the pack's wires. A NiMH pack can push a lot of current through a short and get hot. Insulate every joint.
    • Never wire anything with the pack plugged in.
    • Use this on the NiMH pack it was built for. A LiPo pack needs different charging and handling rules. Ask before you try one.
    • If soldering: a teacher present, a stand for the iron, eye protection and a fume extractor.
    • The red 5V wire stays unconnected. Feeding pack voltage into it can damage the board.

    10What I would change

    • Reset the reconnect wait when Wi-Fi rejoins. Trial 4's 38-second gap is too long during a run.
    • The 6.6 V swap line came from one pack. Packs age. I'd log each pack by its number and set a line per pack.
    • The demo video should be the real rig. A screen recording proves the logic, not the wiring.
    • The M5Stack adds weight to the robot. For a light robot, a smaller board would be better.
