Lesson planCheck one claim with Python

A 2-hour evening lesson for adult beginners. They read a 17-line program that opens a real CSV file. They break it on purpose, then change it to answer their own question.

  • Learners: 16 working adults in my evening class. Most are new to code. Some use spreadsheets every day.
  • Where they are: lesson 4 of 6. They know print, variables, if, for loops, lists and functions. Today is their first dictionary and their first file.
  • Time: 7.00 pm to 9.00 pm, with a 10-minute break.
  • Devices: their own laptops, with their own Google accounts, in Google Colab. Backup: the worksheet page, which runs the starter with no login.
  • Files: the starter notebook and the CSV, sent by email the day before. Also on the worksheet page.
  • Printouts: one worksheet each. One error card per table.
  • Seating: pairs, but every learner types in their own notebook.

By the end, learners can

  • Read a CSV file into a dictionary of lists with the csv module.
  • Read a traceback from the bottom up, and find where the bad data came from.
  • Say why text and numbers need converting, using their own error as the example.
  • Change a program to answer a new question about the same file.

Success criteria

  1. I wrote all three predictions before I ran the code.
  2. I can point to the line that turns text into a number. I can say what breaks without it.
  3. My M2 program gives 0, 3 and 8.
  4. I asked the file my own question. I can explain the answer.

The lesson

  1. 7.00 to 7.10 (10 min)Set up. Open Colab. File, then Upload notebook. Upload the CSV in the Files panel. Run the first cell. I walk round. Anyone stuck after 5 minutes moves to the worksheet page and carries on.
  2. 7.10 to 7.20 (10 min)Hook. On the screen: "2024 was Singapore's warmest year on record." I ask: "How would you check that? What would you need?" We list it on the board: the data, its source, its dates, a way to average.
  3. 7.20 to 7.35 (15 min)Predict. Laptop lids half down. Each learner writes P1 to P3 alone for 8 minutes. Then pairs compare for 7 minutes. Pairs mark any answer they disagree on.
  4. 7.35 to 7.45 (10 min)Run. Run the starter. Circle right predictions. I ask for the P3 guesses aloud, smallest gap first. Then we look at 1.67.
  5. 7.45 to 8.05 (20 min)Investigate. I1, I2 and I3. Each one: predict, change, run, then put the line back. After I1, I model reading the traceback from the bottom line up, on the screen.
  6. 8.05 to 8.15 (10 min)Break. Notebooks stay open.
  7. 8.15 to 8.40 (25 min)Modify. M1, then M2. At 8.30, everyone pastes their M2 output into the class chat.
  8. 8.40 to 8.52 (12 min)Make. Each pair writes one question for the file, then answers it. Two pairs share theirs on the screen.
  9. 8.52 to 9.00 (8 min)Exit. One sentence each, in the chat: "A claim I could now check, and the file I'd need." Then download the notebook from the File menu to keep it. The CSV upload will vanish when Colab's machine is recycled.

Check: 10 + 10 + 15 + 10 + 20 + 10 + 25 + 12 + 8 = 120 minutes.

Support ideas

Slow typists

  • Nobody types the starter. It comes in the notebook.
  • The Investigate tasks each change one line. M1 is three lines. M2 is six. I show M2's first line on the screen at 8.25 for anyone who needs it.
  • Many adults type with two fingers and feel slow. I say early that speed doesn't matter tonight. Reading does.

Typos and errors

One card per table:

Read the last line of the error first. It names the problem. Then read upwards to find the line numbers.

  • KeyError: that key isn't in the dictionary. Check spelling, and text versus number.
  • TypeError ... 'int' and 'str': a number met text. Look for a missing float() or int().
  • FileNotFoundError: upload the CSV again. Check the file name matches exactly.
  • IndentationError or SyntaxError: look at the line it names, and the one above it.

A hand goes up. Before I look, I ask "what does the last line say?" Usually that's enough.

Checking that everyone codes

  • Every learner has their own notebook. Pairs talk, but each person types.
  • Predictions are written alone first, on paper, before the pair talks.
  • At 8.30, everyone pastes their M2 output into the class chat. I can see at a glance who has 0, 3 and 8. I can also see who has nothing yet. I go to them first.
  • Each learner writes their own exit sentence. It shows me who can name a file and a claim.

Other learners

  • Spreadsheet experts who finish early: in Make, find the trend since 1984 without looking at cell 5 of my notebook. Then compare.
  • Nervous about being wrong: predictions stay private on paper until they choose to share. I share a wrong prediction of my own first.
  • Work laptops that block Colab or Drive: the worksheet page runs the same starter in the browser, with no login.
  • Small screens or tired eyes at 8 pm: browser zoom, and I make the code on the projector large.

Why PRIMM with adults?

PRIMM stands for Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make. Sue Sentance, Jane Waite and Maria Kallia tested it in a 2019 study. It covered 493 students aged 11 to 14, in 13 schools. They were compared with a control group. The PRIMM learners did better on the post-test.

Note the ages. That study was with children, not adults. I use PRIMM with adults because reading before writing helps my nervous beginners. But I can't point to a study of adults, and I tell my learners that.
