A Python notebook that reads NEA's own monthly temperature file. It checks three things NEA has said about Singapore's warming. Then a PRIMM lesson turns it into a first data lesson for adults.

I'm Hafiz. I teach data and AI to secondary students and to adult learners. This project is for my adult evening class: working adults, most of them new to code. They all have views on the weather. Few have ever checked a claim against the data behind it. So this program checks three claims first, and draws the chart last.

The three claims

All three come from the National Environment Agency (NEA). I picked claims with exact numbers, because exact numbers can be checked.

Claim 1

"In 2024, Singapore's annual average temperature was 28.4 degrees Celsius, making it the warmest year on record (tied with 2019 and 2016)."

NEA, 23 March 2025

Claim 2

"The preceding decade (2015-2024) was Singapore's warmest decade on record at 28.11 degrees Celsius."

NEA, 23 March 2025

Claim 3

"Singapore's annual mean temperature has risen by 0.24 degrees Celsius per decade since 1984."

NEA, 23 March 2026

The data

The file is Surface Air Temperature - Monthly Mean on data.gov.sg. NEA manages it. Each row is one month at the Changi Climate Station: a month like 1982-01 and a mean temperature like 25.9. I downloaded it on 24 October 2026. It had 537 rows, from January 1982 to September 2026. NEA adds a row each month, so a newer copy will be longer.

Contains information from Surface Air Temperature - Monthly Mean accessed on 24 October 2026 from data.gov.sg, which is made available under the terms of the Singapore Open Data Licence version 1.0.

Three things in the data surprised me. Each one is now a question for my class.

  • The file starts in 1982, but NEA's trends start in 1984. The Meteorological Service Singapore's 2025 climate report says why. The climate station has been at Changi Airport since 1984. My first version started in 1982. Its trend came out at 0.22 °C per decade, not 0.24. Reading the footnote changed my code.
  • 2026 has only 9 months. Its mean leaves out November and December, which are cooler months at Changi. So cell 2 drops any year without 12 months.
  • Look at 1997 in cell 3: 28.25 "rounds to" 28.2, not 28.3. The mean is exactly 28.25, and Python rounds an exact tie to the even digit. The round() docs explain it. My adult learners always ask about this.

What it does not show: why. The data shows Changi getting warmer. It says nothing about the cause. That is a different claim, and it needs different evidence.

Is the chart fair?

The line chart's y axis runs from about 26.7 °C to 28.5 °C. It doesn't start at 0. In this module we met charts with a hidden cut axis. It makes a change look big. So is this one a trick?

I say it's fair here, for two reasons. First, 0 °C means nothing in Singapore's weather. A bar chart from 0 would squash a 1.7-degree range into a flat row. Second, the axis is labelled, and the numbers are printed above it. matplotlib picks this kind of range for line charts by itself. For bar charts, it starts at 0. I ask my class to try plt.bar in cell 6. Then they argue about which is more fair.

My share link is this page. It runs the whole notebook in any browser, with no account. To run the real thing in Google Colab:

Limits, and what I would change

  • One station is not all of Singapore. Changi is NEA's climate station, but other places may have warmed more or less.
  • The monthly means are rounded to 0.1 °C. So 2024 "beating" 2019 by 0.01 °C means nothing. NEA calls it a tie, and so should we.
  • A straight-line trend is a summary. It doesn't explain the bumps, like the warm years of 1997 and 1998.
  • Two learners asked why we don't use pandas. It would be shorter. I chose the csv module so the loop is in plain sight. Next time I'd add a last cell that does the same in pandas. Then they can compare.
