Seven prompts, with my red pen

Each prompt ran twice in Claude Sonnet 5.5 on 24 October 2026, in a fresh session. I read every answer as I would a new trainer's lesson plan.

The lines in italics are Claude's own words, copied from the runs. My notes are in red. Every prompt is about one lesson: loops in Scratch with the repeat block, for 30 Primary 5 pupils.

1. A ladder of questions

For the trainer who asks "any questions?" and hears silence.

I teach a coding enrichment class of 30 Primary 5 pupils in Singapore, aged 11. Today's lesson is about loops in Scratch, using the "repeat" block. Write 6 questions I can ask during the lesson. Go from easy recall to "why" and "what if" questions. For each, add one likely wrong answer a pupil might give, and how I could respond kindly.

Run 1 Changed

Good ladder. The kind responses sound like a real teacher. One question asked whether a "play sound" block goes inside or after the loop.

Name the exact block. Scratch has two. With [start sound], the loop does not wait for the sound to finish. I wrote [play sound until done].

Run 2 Changed

Likely wrong answer: "Repeat 4, turn 90 degrees." (Missing the move block, or using repeat 360.)

That pupil is nearly right. Calling it wrong would sting. I changed the response: praise the half that works, then ask what the cat still needs to do.

2. Misconceptions, with a quick check

So the trainer knows what to look for before it happens.

List 5 common mistakes or misconceptions that 11-year-olds have when they first use the "repeat" block in Scratch. For each, give a 1-minute check I can do on the projector to find out who has it.

Run 1 Changed

They put repeat 10 [move 10 steps] with no wait, and are confused when the sprite just jumps.

Not quite. Each pass of a loop waits for the next frame, so it shows as a very quick slide. Run it and see.

Another item said a [go to x: 0 y: 0] inside the loop means "nothing visibly builds up".

With the pen down, it draws a plus sign: four lines out from the centre. I ran it. Pupils will see that.

Run 2 Changed

Without a wait block, repeat 10 [move 10 steps] looks like one jump.

The same slip as run 1. Two runs agreeing does not make it right. The other four checks were good, and I kept them.

6. Rehearse your questioning

For new trainers who fix the code for the pupil. Practise asking, not telling.

Pretend you are a Primary 5 pupil who is stuck because your Scratch sprite only moves once when you click the green flag. I am the teacher. Answer my questions the way a real 11-year-old might, in short sentences. Do not work out the problem until I ask a question that helps you see it. Start by telling me what is wrong, in one or two sentences.

Run 2 Kept

Almost the same words. It sounds like a pupil, and it waits for the teacher.

I tested the first reply only. Try a whole conversation yourself before you hand it to a trainee.

What the seven taught me

  • Claude was right about Scratch more often than not. Its slips were about timing and what shows on screen, the things you only see by running it.
  • A word limit is a request, not a promise. Count.
  • Claude follows your words closely. A gap in your instructions stays a gap.
  • The best use was rehearsal. A nervous trainer can practise questions before facing 30 pupils.
