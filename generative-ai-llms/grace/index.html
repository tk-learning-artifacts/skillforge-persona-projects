A guide for new trainers, on one AI tool

Claude stays on my desk

How a new trainer can use Claude to plan Primary 5 Scratch lessons, and why the pupils never touch it.

I'm Grace. I taught for twenty years. Now I mentor new trainers. Many of them teach coding enrichment in primary schools, in Scratch, to classes of about 30. This year they keep asking me the same thing. Should I use AI? I was cautious. So before I answered, I tested Claude on the jobs they actually do. This guide is what I found.

Claude is for the trainer. Pupils never type into it.

Anthropic's terms say users must be at least 18. My pupils are 11. When Claude's words go on the projector, they are words I have already read and checked.

My routine: ask, check, change, then use

New trainers like a routine. So do I. Here is the one I ask them to follow, every time.

  1. Ask

    Say who the class is, what you want and how long. "For 30 P5 pupils. 6 questions. Under 80 words."

  2. Check

    Run every script in Scratch yourself. Read every line aloud, as if to the child.

  3. Change

    Fix it with a pen, on paper or on screen. Keep a note of what you changed.

  4. Then use

    Only checked words reach a pupil. If you could not check it, leave it out.

What my red pen found

I ran 7 prompts, twice each, in Claude Sonnet 5.5 on 24 October 2026. That gave me 14 answers to read. Here is what happened to them.

Most of the changes were small. But they were the kind a pupil would notice. Three examples:

  • In both runs of one prompt, Claude said a loop with no wait block makes the sprite jump once. In Scratch, each pass of a loop waits for the next frame. So ten moves show as a very quick slide.
  • An exit ticket fixed a jumping cat by putting the up and down blocks in one loop. It called a wait block "a bonus idea, not required". It is required. Without it, the cat goes up and down within one frame, and never seems to move.
  • One exit ticket question argued with itself halfway through. Claude spotted it and said so at the end. I did not use that one.

So Claude saved my trainers time. It did not save them from checking. A new trainer who runs every script will catch all of these. One who doesn't will teach them.

How it meets the brief

  • Accurate about what AI can do today. I say what Claude did well and where it slipped, with the answers to show it. I don't claim it can see your class or run Scratch. It cannot.
  • Prompts tested and classroom-ready. Each prompt was run twice. Every answer was read, checked in Scratch where it had code, and marked kept, changed or not used.
  • Covers risks and how to reduce them. Page 2 of the guide covers age limits, pupil data, wrong details, tone, and trainers leaning on it.
  • Clear without a technical background. No code beyond Scratch block names. Many of my new trainers came from other jobs, and I wrote it for them.

What I would change

Two runs a prompt is not many. I tested one topic, the repeat block. A trainer teaching broadcasts or variables should test their own prompts the same way. I tested only the first reply of the role-play prompt, not a whole conversation.

And I have not seen it work for a whole term. Testing prompts is one thing. Watching a nervous new trainer use it at 11pm, the night before a lesson, is another. I would like to sit with two of them next term and see what they actually keep.
