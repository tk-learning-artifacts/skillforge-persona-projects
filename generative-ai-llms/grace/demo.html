Predict, then check

A one-minute video of the moment Claude appears in a Primary 5 lesson. Pupils predict first. Claude's explanation comes second. Scratch has the last word.

61 seconds, no sound. The captions are in the picture, and the transcript is below. The screen is re-created and the class is made up. Claude's words are copied from my test runs on 24 October 2026.

Why the order matters

If Claude speaks first, pupils stop thinking. They copy the confident voice on the big screen. So pupils commit to a guess on their whiteboards before anything appears. Then Claude is one more voice to test, like a classmate's answer. Then we run the script and see who was right.

I also show my checking. Pupils see their trainer read an AI answer and run it before trusting it. That habit is worth more to them than the triangle.

The whole lesson, 60 minutes

30 Primary 5 pupils, in 15 pairs, on school laptops. They use the Scratch editor without signing in, and save to the laptop. Claude runs only on the trainer's computer.

  1. 0 to 5 min. Settle. Laptops on, Scratch open. No sign-in.
  2. 5 to 10 min. Warm-up from the question ladder, prompt 1. Think, pair, share for the first two questions.
  3. 10 to 16 min. Predict, then check, on the projector. This is the video. Whiteboards up, tally, Claude's explanation, then run it.
  4. 16 to 20 min. Mini lesson: inside or outside the repeat block. Two quick checks from prompt 2, with thumbs up or down.
  5. 20 to 40 min. Pairs build. First a square with one repeat block. Then a triangle: find the angle yourselves. Early finishers try a hexagon.
  6. 40 to 48 min. Two pairs show their triangle. I ask the "why" and "what if" questions from the ladder.
  7. 48 to 55 min. Exit ticket, the checked version from prompt 4, on paper.
  8. 55 to 60 min. Save to the laptop. Pack up.

Check: 5 + 5 + 6 + 4 + 20 + 8 + 7 + 5 = 60 minutes. Claude appears for 6 of them.

Before the lesson

  • Run prompt 7 the night before. Read the answer. Run the script in Scratch. Cut anything that gives away the triangle.
  • Keep the checked answer in a text file. In class, show the file, not a live chat. Nothing new appears on the projector.
  • Tell pupils plainly: this is an AI tool for grown-ups. They will check it, the way they check each other.
