Eight tested prompts

1. Mean or median, with a worked example

3 of 3 passedWeek 2: averages
I am an adult learner in a beginner data course. Explain the difference between the mean and the median. Use one example about the monthly salaries of 9 people in a small office, where one person earns far more than the rest. Give the numbers, work out both, and say which one better describes a typical worker. Keep it under 200 words.
Check
Every total, mean and median is right.
Result
All right. Totals of 56,300, 55,200 and 51,000 gave means of 6,256, 6,133 and 5,667. I redid each sum. The medians were right too.
From run 1

Eight of the nine people earn between $2,800 and $3,800, yet the mean is $6,256, a figure nobody near the middle earns.

In class
Learners redo the sum in Sheets first. Then they read Claude's answer. Each run makes up new salaries, so pairs can compare.

2. A practice dataset with one outlier

3 of 3 passedWeek 2: cleaning data
Make a practice dataset for a beginner spreadsheet class. Give 15 rows of made-up daily sales for one drink stall at a hawker centre, as CSV with the columns date, day, cups_sold, rain (yes or no). Use the dates 1 to 15 March 2027, with the correct day of the week for each. Put in one clear outlier. After the table, say which row is the outlier. Say clearly that the data is made up.
Check
Weekdays right. Outlier named. Marked as made up.
Result
1 March 2027 is a Monday, and all 45 rows had the right day. The outliers were 412, 520 and 412 cups. All 3 runs said the data was made up.
From run 2

The outlier is data row 10 (not counting the header): 10 March 2027, Wednesday, 520 cups.

Watch for
Run 3 put the "made up" line after the table. A learner who copies only the table loses the label. I have them type MADE-UP DATA in cell A1.

3. Quiz me, one question at a time

3 of 3 passedAny week: revision
Quiz me on reading bar charts and line charts. Ask one question at a time. Wait for my answer before you give feedback. Start easy and get harder. Do not give the answer until I have tried.
Check
One question only. No answer given away.
Result
All 3 runs asked one easy question about books read by four students, and stopped.
Watch for
It drew the bar charts with text characters. On a narrow phone screen these can wrap and break. Use a laptop. I tested only the first turn, not a whole quiz.

4. Plan a fact check, without a verdict

3 of 3 passedWeek 3: checking claims
A news headline says: "Singapore's population hit a record high this year." I want to check it myself. Do not tell me whether it is true. Instead, list the 4 questions I should ask to check it, and name the official Singapore source where I could find each answer.
Check
No verdict. Real, official sources.
Result
No run said true or false. All named SingStat and the Population in Brief report, which both exist. All asked which measure the headline meant: total, resident or citizen.
From run 1

A "record high" could apply to one and not the others.

In class
Pairs answer the 4 questions using the real sources. This is the prompt I like best. Claude plans, the learner checks.

5. A number we can check

0 of 3 currentWeek 3: the trap
What is Singapore's total population? Give the figure and the date it refers to.
Check
Gives the latest official figure.
Result
All 3 runs said about 6.11 million, as at June 2025. SingStat now gives 6.21 million, as at end-June 2026. Every run gave the date and said to check SingStat.
From run 2

A 2026 edition has likely been released since, and it's past my knowledge cutoff.

In class
I use it on purpose. Learners find the newer number in two minutes. They see that a dated answer is a lead to follow, not a fact.

6a. Find studies

2 passed, 1 with a problemWeek 3: the second trap
Give me 3 peer-reviewed studies on whether students who use AI chatbots for homework learn less. For each, give the authors, year, journal and DOI.
Check
Every study is real and not retracted. Details match the record.
Result
All 3 runs named the same 3 papers. I looked up all 9 DOIs on Crossref. Authors, journals and volumes matched. In its notes, run 2 also cited a meta-analysis by Wang and Fan as positive evidence. That paper was retracted on 22 April 2026.
From run 1

I'm giving the DOIs from memory and haven't checked them against a live database, so please confirm each one at doi.org before citing it.

6b. Find studies, with my fix

1 passed, 1 with a problem, 1 failed
Give me 3 peer-reviewed studies on whether students who use AI chatbots for homework learn less. For each, give the authors, year, journal and DOI. If you are not sure a study or a detail is real, say so clearly instead of guessing. I will check every DOI myself.
Result
Each answer now rated its own confidence, item by item. That part worked. But run 1 listed the retracted Wang and Fan paper as one of its 3 studies. Run 2 shortened one title, writing "generative AI" for "generative artificial intelligence". Run 3 was clean.
From run 1

Confidence: Moderate. I'm fairly sure the paper exists, but I'm unsure of the article number and the DOI. Check this one most carefully.

What I learnt
The DOI was right. The paper exists. It was retracted. Claude's doubt pointed at the wrong thing. A better prompt cannot fix knowledge the model does not have. So the check stays with the learner: open the paper's page and look for "Retracted".

7. Explain a spreadsheet formula

3 of 3 passedWeek 4: formulas
In Google Sheets, column B (rows 2 to 16) has cups sold and column D (rows 2 to 16) has "yes" or "no" for rain. Write one formula for the average cups sold on rainy days only. Then explain each part of the formula in plain words for a beginner.
Check
Formula right. Each part explained.
Result
All 3 gave =AVERAGEIF(D2:D16, "yes", B2:B16). That matches Google's own help page: the range to check, the condition, then the range to average.
Watch for
Run 1 added that the match ignores capital letters. I did not check that. Test it in a sheet before you teach it.
In class
Use it with the stall data from prompt 2. Learners check the formula on the rainy rows by hand.

8. Summarise, with a quote for each point

3 with a problemWeek 5: reading reports
Summarise this made-up memo in 3 bullet points for a beginner. After each bullet, quote the exact words from the memo that support it. Memo: [paste the text here]
Check
Every quote is word for word. Each bullet is one clear point.
Result
I tested it on a short made-up memo about a room change. All 11 quotes matched the memo exactly. I checked them with a script. But all 3 runs packed two unrelated points into bullet 3: a broken coffee machine and the rule for missing a class.
From run 1

Two other practical notes: the coffee machine is out, and absences need advance notice.

What I learnt
That was my fault. The memo had four points, and I asked for three. Next time I will write "up to 4 bullet points, one idea each". I have not tested that version yet.

What the eight have in common

  • The prompts that passed ask Claude to do work: explain, make, plan, quiz. Learners can check that work.
  • The prompts that failed ask Claude to know facts: a new figure, a paper's status.
  • Every good prompt names who it is for, what to make, and a limit. "For a beginner." "15 rows." "Under 200 words."
  • A prompt can ask Claude to be careful. It cannot give Claude facts it does not have.
