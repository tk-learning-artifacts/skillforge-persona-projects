Why do apples go brown?

The session 2 fair test. Four apple slices, one change each, scored at 0, 10 and 20 minutes. Nothing is eaten.

You need

For each of 8 groups:

  • 4 slices from one apple, about the same size. An adult cuts them.
  • A paper plate labelled A, B, C and D.
  • Two small cups: one of tap water, one of bottled lemon juice.
  • A square of cling film, a colour scale card and a results sheet.

For the adult: one sharp knife and a cutting board, used only at the front bench.

Safety steps

Read these out before anything is handed out

  • No tasting, at any point. These apples go in the bin at the end. I tell pupils why: we will make food to eat in session 4, with clean hands and clean tools.
  • Only adults use the sharp knife, at the front bench, with pupils seated.
  • Check the allergy letters first. A pupil with a fruit allergy does not handle the apple. They score and record instead.
  • Lemon juice stings in eyes and cuts. Pupils with a cut on their hand use a spoon to dip.
  • Wipe drips at once, so nobody slips. Wash hands at the end.

Method

  1. Predict. Which slice will stay whitest? Write why.
  2. Leave slice A alone. It is the control: the one we compare the others with.
  3. Dip slice B in water for 30 seconds.
  4. Dip slice C in lemon juice for 30 seconds.
  5. Wrap slice D tightly in cling film.
  6. Score every slice now, at 0 minutes. Use the colour scale.
  7. Score again at 10 minutes and at 20 minutes.

Fair test, in pupil words

  • We change: what we put on the slice.
  • We keep the same: the apple, the size of the slice, the plate, the room, the time.
  • We measure: how brown it is, from 0 to 3.

Why dip B in water? If C stays white, someone will say "it was just because it was wet". Slice B answers that.

Why it happens

Cutting an apple lets air reach the inside. The air switches on an enzyme in the apple called polyphenol oxidase. The enzyme turns the cut surface brown. Lemon juice slows it. Its vitamin C, called ascorbic acid, reacts with the air first. Cling film slows it a little by keeping air away, but it never fits perfectly.

For P5, I say it more simply. "Air plus something in the apple makes brown. Lemon juice gets in the way."

Lemon juice worked best in all 8 groups. Two groups scored their water slice browner than the control. When we talked it through, they had judged it under the window, where the light was different. So we added "score at the same bench" to the fair test list. I was glad it happened. It showed them why we keep things the same.

The link to food safety

This is the question I build the session around.

"Is the brown apple unsafe? Is the white one safe?"

Most pupils say brown means bad. Then I ask what made it brown. It was air, not germs. And the white slice? It was touched by eight hands, so we cannot know. You cannot see germs. Colour does not tell you if food is safe. Clean hands, cold food and clean tools do. That is where session 3 begins.

In session 4, pupils use what they found. The apple in their breakfast cup gets a lemon juice dip.
