Breakfast Cups

Four one-hour sessions for a Primary 5 class. Pupils plan a breakfast with My Healthy Plate and test why apples go brown. They learn the 4Cs of food safety. Then they make and eat their own breakfast cup.

I'm Grace. I taught for twenty years and now mentor new trainers. I built this programme with a trainer in her first year. So it is written for someone running food in a classroom for the first time. Most of the work is routines: who does what, when, and how we check. The food is simple on purpose. Nothing is cooked, so the hour can go into habits.

  • Audience: one P5 class of 32 pupils, aged 10 and 11, in 8 groups of 4.
  • Time: four sessions of 60 minutes, one a week, in a double period. A 15-minute post-quiz the morning after session 4.
  • Room: the science room, which has 4 sinks and wipeable benches.
  • Adults: the trainer and me in every session. One parent volunteer for session 4.
  • The food: rolled oats, plain low-fat yoghurt, banana and apple, with plain water to drink.

Learning outcomes

By the end, each pupil can:

  1. Plan a breakfast with wholegrains, protein and fruit, using My Healthy Plate.
  2. Wash their hands with soap for 20 seconds, covering every part.
  3. Run a fair test and say which one thing they changed.
  4. Name the 4Cs and use each one while making food.

I wrote the success criteria in pupil words and put them on the board each week. "I can say which part of my cup is the wholegrain." "I can wash for 20 seconds without being reminded."

The evidence behind it

I kept to Singapore's own advice, so pupils hear the same words at home, in school and at the clinic.

  • My Healthy Plate: half the plate fruit and vegetables, a quarter wholegrains, a quarter lean meat and others. HealthHub's breakfast example says the fruit and vegetables can be optional at breakfast. It lists rolled oats as a wholegrain and unflavoured low-fat yoghurt as a protein.
  • The Singapore Food Agency's 4Cs: cleanliness and hygiene, control of temperature, cook food well, and counter cross-contamination. SFA names the danger zone as 5°C to 60°C, where germs multiply fastest.
  • HealthHub's advice for children: rinsing with water is not enough. Rub all surfaces of the hands with soap and water for 20 seconds.
  • For the science: cut apple browns when air reaches an enzyme in the fruit, polyphenol oxidase. Lemon juice slows it. This comes from the University of Wisconsin Extension.

The four sessions

Each session is planned to the minute on the sessions page, with the questions I ask.

  • Session 1What's in a good breakfast? Pre-quiz. Sort local breakfast foods onto a plate. Design a breakfast cup on paper.
  • Session 2Why do apples go brown? The apple browning fair test. No tasting.
  • Session 3The 4Cs. A timed handwash check. A hunt for six planted hazards. Jobs for next week.
  • Session 4Make it, eat it, clean up. Each pupil makes and eats a breakfast cup, with the 4Cs and their job.

The food science demo

Each group tests four apple slices: one left alone, one dipped in water, one dipped in lemon juice, and one wrapped in cling film. They check them at 0, 10 and 20 minutes against a colour scale. The demo page has the set-up, the fair test, the safety steps and our results.

The question I care about comes after the test. A pupil always asks if the brown apple has gone bad. Brown is the air at work. It tells you nothing about germs, and a slice that looks perfect can still carry germs from dirty hands. That is the bridge into the 4Cs.

The routines that keep it safe

Clean

Everyone washes for 20 seconds before touching food. A partner counts. Benches are wiped before and after.

Control temperature

Yoghurt stays in a cooler box with ice packs until a group is ready. A pupil timer keeper has a clipboard. Every cup is eaten within 30 minutes.

Cook well

Nothing is cooked. We talk about why eggs and chicken must be cooked through, and SFA's advice to cook food above 75°C.

Counter cross-contamination

Fruit has its own boards. Each pupil has their own spoon. Serving spoons never touch a mouth.

Before any food comes in

  • A letter home two weeks ahead lists every ingredient and asks about allergies. No reply, no food: that pupil gets water and fruit we have checked.
  • The room is nut-free. For a dairy allergy, the parent agrees a soy yoghurt. For a pupil who avoids oats, the parent agrees a wholegrain swap.
  • Only adults use sharp knives. Pupils cut banana with plastic knives.
  • The apples in the fair test are never eaten. They go in the bin.

Assessment results

My class, the trainer and every number on the results page are made up, like me. The quiz is real, and you can take it.

I used three measures. The same 8-question quiz before session 1 and the morning after session 4. A handwash checklist, ticked by an adult in sessions 3 and 4. And a three-face exit ticket on how pupils felt about making food. The mean quiz score went from 2.1 to 6.4 out of 8. Full handwashes went from 13 of 32 pupils to 27 of 31. The results page shows each question, and the one I am still not happy with.

How it meets the rubric

  • Accurate, current content. Every rule comes from HealthHub or SFA, checked in October 2026, and linked on the page where it is used.
  • Clear learning outcomes. Four outcomes, written again as pupil success criteria. Each one is tested by the quiz or the handwash checklist.
  • Safe, well-managed activities. Allergy letters, a nut-free room, adult-only sharp knives, a cooler box, and a pupil with a timer. Every pupil has a job, so nobody waits with nothing to do.
  • Suits the audience. Ten-year-olds can make this cup with no heat. The breakfast foods are ones they know from home and hawker stalls. The quiz is read aloud, so reading is not what it tests.

What I would change

Question 4 asks for the danger zone, 5°C to 60°C. Two thirds of the class got it right the morning after. I doubt they will in a month. A number on a poster is not the same as a habit. Next time I would drop the numbers for P5 and test the habit instead: "The yoghurt has been out for 40 minutes. What do you do?"

Session 4 also ran 5 minutes over at clean-up. Washing 32 sets of bowls takes longer than you think. Next time, the parent volunteer starts washing while pupils are still eating.
